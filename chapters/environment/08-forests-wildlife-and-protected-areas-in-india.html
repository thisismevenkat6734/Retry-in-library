1. Introduction to Forests in India

A forest is a terrestrial ecosystem dominated by trees and other vegetation, supporting diverse plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms.

తెలుగు: చెట్లు, మొక్కలు, జంతువులు, శిలీంధ్రాలు మరియు సూక్ష్మజీవులతో కూడిన ముఖ్యమైన భూసంబంధ పర్యావరణ వ్యవస్థను అడవి అంటారు.

Importance of Forests

  • Biodiversity: Forests provide habitats for numerous species.
  • Climate regulation: Influence temperature, rainfall patterns and local climate.
  • Carbon storage: Store carbon in trees, vegetation and soil.
  • Soil conservation: Reduce soil erosion and help maintain soil fertility.
  • Water cycle: Support infiltration, groundwater recharge and watershed processes.
  • Livelihoods: Provide food, fuelwood, fodder, timber and non-timber forest products.
  • Ecological stability: Support nutrient cycling and ecosystem functioning.

Forest Cover and Tree Cover

In Indian forest assessment terminology, forest cover generally refers to land of one hectare or more with tree canopy density of 10% or more, irrespective of ownership or legal status.

Tree cover refers to tree patches outside the mapped forest-cover category, including smaller patches and trees outside forests.

Forest Cover అనేది నిర్దిష్ట విస్తీర్ణం మరియు చెట్ల కిరీట సాంద్రత ఆధారంగా అంచనా వేయబడుతుంది. Tree Coverలో అడవుల వెలుపల ఉన్న చిన్న చెట్ల సమూహాలు మరియు ఇతర చెట్లు కూడా చేరవచ్చు.

Exam note: Forest cover is an assessment category; it does not automatically mean that every such area is legally notified forest land or natural forest.

2. Major Forest Types in India

India has varied forest types because of differences in rainfall, temperature, altitude, soil and geography.

A. Tropical Evergreen Forests – ఉష్ణమండల సతత హరిత అడవులు

  • Found in areas with very high rainfall and warm temperatures.
  • Trees remain green throughout the year.
  • Vegetation is dense and multilayered.
  • Common in the Western Ghats, Northeast India and Andaman and Nicobar Islands.
  • Examples of trees: ebony, mahogany and rosewood in suitable regions.

అధిక వర్షపాతం ఉన్న ప్రాంతాల్లో సంవత్సరం పొడవునా పచ్చగా ఉండే దట్టమైన అడవులను సతత హరిత అడవులు అంటారు.

B. Tropical Moist Deciduous Forests – తేమతో కూడిన ఆకురాల్చే అడవులు

  • Found in regions with substantial rainfall and a distinct dry season.
  • Trees shed some or most leaves during the dry season.
  • Common trees include teak, sal and shisham in suitable regions.
  • Found in parts of eastern India, central India and the Himalayan foothills.

C. Tropical Dry Deciduous Forests – పొడి ఆకురాల్చే అడవులు

  • Occur in areas with lower rainfall than moist deciduous forests.
  • Trees shed leaves during the dry season to reduce water loss.
  • Found in parts of central and southern India.
  • Teak and other drought-tolerant species may occur.

D. Tropical Thorn Forests – ముళ్ల అడవులు

  • Found in dry and semi-arid regions.
  • Vegetation includes thorny shrubs and small trees.
  • Plants often have small leaves, thorns and deep roots.
  • Common in Rajasthan, Gujarat and parts of the Deccan Plateau.
  • Examples: Acacia, Prosopis and Capparis.

E. Montane Forests – పర్వత అడవులు

  • Found in mountainous regions at different altitudes.
  • Vegetation changes with altitude and temperature.
  • Lower slopes may have broad-leaved forests.
  • Higher elevations may have temperate forests and coniferous trees.
  • Examples include oak, pine, deodar and fir in suitable Himalayan zones.

F. Mangrove Forests – మడ అడవులు

  • Found in tidal coastal areas, estuaries and river deltas.
  • Plants tolerate saline or brackish water and waterlogged soils.
  • Some mangroves have specialised aerial roots or breathing roots.
  • Protect coastlines from erosion and reduce the impact of storm surges.
  • Provide nursery habitats for fish, crustaceans and other organisms.
  • Important Indian example: Sundarbans.

సముద్రపు ఆటుపోట్లు ప్రభావితం చేసే తీర ప్రాంతాలు, నదీ ముఖద్వారాలు మరియు డెల్టాల్లో పెరిగే ఉప్పునీటిని తట్టుకునే అడవులను మడ అడవులు అంటారు.

G. Littoral and Swamp Forests

  • Occur in coastal and freshwater wetland environments.
  • Include vegetation adapted to waterlogged conditions.
  • Support fish, birds, amphibians and wetland biodiversity.

3. Wildlife of India – భారతదేశ వన్యప్రాణులు

Wildlife includes undomesticated animals, plants and other organisms living in natural ecosystems. Wildlife conservation protects species, habitats and ecological relationships.

సహజ పర్యావరణ వ్యవస్థల్లో నివసించే అడవి జంతువులు, మొక్కలు మరియు ఇతర జీవులను వన్యప్రాణులుగా పరిగణిస్తారు.

Major Wildlife Groups

  • Mammals: Tiger, elephant, lion, rhinoceros, deer and primates.
  • Birds: Great Indian bustard, hornbills, cranes, vultures and waterbirds.
  • Reptiles: Crocodiles, turtles, snakes and lizards.
  • Amphibians: Frogs, toads and salamanders.
  • Fish: Freshwater and marine fish species.
  • Invertebrates: Butterflies, bees, beetles, molluscs and many other groups.

Endemic Wildlife

Endemic species are naturally restricted to a particular geographical region.

  • Nilgiri tahr – Western Ghats.
  • Lion-tailed macaque – Western Ghats.
  • Nicobar megapode – Nicobar Islands.
  • Several amphibians and plants are restricted to specific Indian mountain and island habitats.

Threatened Wildlife

Species may become threatened due to habitat loss, poaching, pollution, invasive species, climate change and human-wildlife conflict.

The IUCN Red List assesses extinction risk using categories such as Critically Endangered, Endangered and Vulnerable.

4. Protected Areas in India

Protected areas are geographically defined areas managed for long-term conservation of nature, biodiversity and associated ecosystem services.

A. National Parks – జాతీయ ఉద్యానవనాలు

  • Established to protect wildlife, habitats and ecosystems.
  • Activities are regulated under the Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972.
  • Hunting is prohibited except as permitted under specific legal provisions.
  • Grazing and resource-use rights are generally more restricted than in many sanctuaries.
  • Boundaries and legal status are formally notified.

B. Wildlife Sanctuaries – వన్యప్రాణి అభయారణ్యాలు

  • Declared to protect wild animals and their habitats.
  • Some rights may continue subject to settlement and regulation under law.
  • Activities are controlled to protect wildlife.
  • They may protect birds, mammals, reptiles, wetlands and other habitats.

C. Conservation Reserves

  • Declared on government-owned land.
  • May adjoin national parks and sanctuaries or connect protected areas.
  • Help conserve landscapes, corridors and habitats.
  • Local participation is encouraged through the relevant conservation reserve management arrangements.

D. Community Reserves

  • Can be declared on private or community land voluntarily conserved by individuals or communities.
  • Support community participation in wildlife conservation.
  • Help protect habitats outside conventional government protected areas.

E. Biosphere Reserves

Biosphere reserves promote biodiversity conservation along with sustainable development, research and education.

ZonePurpose
CoreStrict protection of ecosystems and biodiversity.
BufferResearch, education and compatible regulated activities.
TransitionSustainable economic and community activities.

Some biosphere reserves are recognised under UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere Programme. National designation and UNESCO recognition are related but not identical processes.

F. Tiger Reserves

  • Established under the framework of Project Tiger and the Wildlife (Protection) Act.
  • Include core and buffer areas.
  • Focus on tiger populations, prey species and habitat protection.
  • Also benefit many other species and ecosystem processes.

G. Elephant Reserves

  • Landscape-level areas identified for elephant conservation.
  • Support elephant habitats, migration routes and corridors.
  • Help address habitat fragmentation and human-elephant conflict.

H. Marine Protected Areas

  • Protect coral reefs, mangroves, seagrass beds, coastal wetlands and marine species.
  • Support breeding, feeding and nursery habitats.
  • Examples include Gulf of Mannar and marine protected areas in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

6. Tiger Conservation in India

Project Tiger

  • Launched on 1 April 1973.
  • Its main aim is to maintain viable tiger populations in natural habitats.
  • Promotes habitat protection, anti-poaching measures and scientific monitoring.
  • Supports tiger reserves across suitable landscapes.

National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA)

  • Established as a statutory body under the Wildlife (Protection) Act framework.
  • Provides oversight and direction for tiger conservation.
  • Supports tiger reserve management and monitoring.
  • Works with state governments and other agencies.

Core and Buffer Areas in Tiger Reserves

  • Core: Critical tiger habitat with stronger conservation priority.
  • Buffer: Surrounding area managed to support conservation along with compatible livelihood and land-use activities.

Importance of Tiger Conservation

  • Tigers are large carnivores and can influence prey populations.
  • Protecting tiger landscapes also protects forests, water sources and many other species.
  • Healthy habitats support ecological connectivity.
  • Conservation can support local livelihoods through carefully managed nature-based activities.

Major Threats to Tigers

  • Poaching and illegal wildlife trade.
  • Habitat loss and fragmentation.
  • Decline in prey populations.
  • Human-tiger conflict.
  • Loss of landscape connectivity.

7. Elephant Conservation in India

Project Elephant

  • Launched in 1992.
  • Focuses on the protection of elephants, their habitats and migration corridors.
  • Supports research, monitoring and management.
  • Addresses human-elephant conflict and elephant welfare.

Importance of Elephant Corridors

  • Allow elephants to move between habitat patches.
  • Support seasonal migration and access to food and water.
  • Reduce isolation of populations.
  • Help maintain genetic exchange between populations.

Human-Elephant Conflict

Conflict may occur when elephants enter farms, settlements or infrastructure areas while moving through landscapes.

  • Protect and restore traditional movement routes.
  • Improve early-warning and community response systems.
  • Use suitable barriers and land-use planning where appropriate.
  • Provide timely response and compensation mechanisms under applicable schemes.
  • Promote community participation and safe behaviour around elephants.

8. Wildlife Corridors and Landscape Conservation

A wildlife corridor is a strip or network of habitat that connects separated habitat patches and allows animals to move between them.

విడిపోయిన అడవి ప్రాంతాలను కలుపుతూ వన్యప్రాణులు ఒక ప్రాంతం నుంచి మరో ప్రాంతానికి వెళ్లేందుకు సహాయపడే సహజ మార్గాలను Wildlife Corridors అంటారు.

Importance of Corridors

  • Allow movement between fragmented habitats.
  • Support breeding and gene flow between populations.
  • Provide access to seasonal food and water.
  • Reduce the ecological effects of habitat fragmentation.
  • Help species adapt to changing environmental conditions.

Landscape-Level Conservation

Landscape conservation considers connected forests, grasslands, rivers, farms, settlements and protected areas as parts of a larger ecological system.

  • Coordinate conservation across administrative boundaries.
  • Maintain connectivity between protected areas.
  • Reduce risks from roads, railways and other infrastructure.
  • Include local communities in land-use planning.

9. Wildlife Conservation Laws in India

Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972

  • Provides the principal legal framework for protection of wild animals, birds and plants in India.
  • Provides for protected areas such as national parks and wildlife sanctuaries.
  • Regulates hunting and wildlife trade.
  • Has been amended over time, including the 2022 amendment.
  • Supports institutions and authorities involved in wildlife conservation.

Forest (Conservation) Act, 1980

  • Originally enacted to regulate diversion of forest land for non-forest purposes.
  • Provides a legal framework for central approval in specified cases.
  • Its title and provisions were amended in 2023; consult the current law for exact scope and provisions.

Environment (Protection) Act, 1986

  • Provides a broad framework for environmental protection.
  • Empowers the Central Government to take measures to protect and improve environmental quality.
  • Supports rules and notifications relating to pollution and environmental management.

Biological Diversity Act, 2002

  • Promotes conservation of biological diversity.
  • Supports sustainable use of biological resources.
  • Provides for fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from biological resources.

11. Forests and Wildlife in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana

Andhra Pradesh

  • Sri Venkateswara National Park: Located in the Seshachalam hill landscape; important for dry deciduous and mixed forest habitats.
  • Papikonda National Park: Located in the Eastern Ghats along the Godavari landscape; protects forest and riverine ecosystems.
  • Rollapadu Wildlife Sanctuary: Known for grassland habitat and conservation of the Great Indian Bustard historically associated with the area.
  • Kolleru Wildlife Sanctuary: Important wetland habitat for resident and migratory birds.
  • Coringa Wildlife Sanctuary: Important mangrove ecosystem near the Godavari estuary.
  • Krishna Wildlife Sanctuary: Protects mangrove and estuarine habitats.
  • Seshachalam Biosphere Reserve: Known for the Eastern Ghats landscape and endemic plant diversity, including red sanders in suitable habitats.

Telangana

  • Kawal Tiger Reserve: Important forest landscape in northern Telangana.
  • Amrabad Tiger Reserve: Located in the Nallamala forest landscape.
  • Eturnagaram Wildlife Sanctuary: Protects forest habitats in the Godavari region.
  • Pakhal Wildlife Sanctuary: Associated with the Pakhal lake and surrounding forest ecosystem.
  • Manjeera Wildlife Sanctuary: Important wetland and bird habitat.
  • Pocharam Wildlife Sanctuary: Includes reservoir and surrounding habitats supporting wildlife and birds.

12. Major Threats to Forests and Wildlife

  • Deforestation: Clearing forests for agriculture, mining, infrastructure and settlements.
  • Habitat fragmentation: Breaking continuous habitats into smaller isolated patches.
  • Poaching: Illegal hunting or capture of wildlife.
  • Illegal wildlife trade: Trade in protected animals, plants and their parts.
  • Forest fires: Can destroy vegetation, habitats and wildlife.
  • Invasive species: Non-native species can displace native biodiversity.
  • Pollution: Contaminates habitats and affects animal health.
  • Climate change: Changes temperature, rainfall, habitats and species distribution.
  • Human-wildlife conflict: Conflict between people and wild animals over crops, livestock, property and safety.

Conservation Measures

  • Strengthen protected-area management.
  • Protect wildlife corridors and natural habitats.
  • Prevent poaching and illegal trade.
  • Restore degraded forests and grasslands.
  • Use scientific monitoring and wildlife research.
  • Encourage community participation.
  • Reduce human-wildlife conflict through early warning and planning.
  • Promote responsible tourism and environmental education.
