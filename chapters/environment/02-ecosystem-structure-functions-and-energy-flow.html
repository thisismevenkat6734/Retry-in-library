1. Meaning of Ecosystem | పర్యావరణ వ్యవస్థ అర్థం

An ecosystem is a functional unit of nature in which living organisms interact with one another and with the non-living environment.

పర్యావరణ వ్యవస్థ (Ecosystem) అనేది జీవులు ఒకదానితో మరొకటి మరియు వాటి నిర్జీవ పరిసరాలతో పరస్పర చర్యలు జరిపే ప్రకృతిలోని క్రియాత్మక వ్యవస్థ.

  • The term ecosystem was introduced by British ecologist Arthur George Tansley in 1935.
  • An ecosystem includes both biotic and abiotic components.
  • Energy flows through ecosystems, while nutrients are recycled.
  • Ecosystems may be natural or artificial.
Exam Point: A.G. Tansley introduced the term Ecosystem in 1935. Ernst Haeckel coined the term Ecology in 1866. Do not confuse these two facts.

Examples of ecosystems

  • Natural terrestrial: Forest, grassland, desert and tundra.
  • Natural aquatic: Pond, lake, river, wetland, estuary and ocean.
  • Artificial: Crop field, aquarium, garden, reservoir and urban park.

Types based on size

  • Micro-ecosystem: Small-scale ecosystem, such as a small water-filled tree hole.
  • Meso-ecosystem: Medium-scale ecosystem, such as a pond or small forest patch.
  • Macro-ecosystem: Large-scale ecosystem, such as a large forest or ocean system.

2. Structure of an Ecosystem | పర్యావరణ వ్యవస్థ నిర్మాణం

The structure of an ecosystem refers to the composition and organisation of its living and non-living components.

A. Abiotic Components | నిర్జీవ అంశాలు

These are the physical and chemical factors that influence organisms.

ComponentExamplesEcological importance
LightSunlightPrimary energy source for most ecosystems.
TemperatureAir and water temperatureInfluences metabolism, growth and distribution.
WaterRainfall, rivers, groundwaterEssential for metabolism and life processes.
SoilTexture, pH, mineralsProvides support, water and nutrients to plants.
AirOxygen, carbon dioxide, nitrogenSupports respiration, photosynthesis and nutrient cycles.
Inorganic nutrientsNitrogen, phosphorus, potassiumRequired for growth and biological functions.

B. Biotic Components | జీవ అంశాలు

These are the living components of an ecosystem. They are commonly grouped according to how they obtain energy and nutrients.

1. Producers (Autotrophs) | ఉత్పాదకులు

  • Producers manufacture organic food from inorganic substances.
  • Most producers use sunlight through photosynthesis.
  • Examples: Green plants, algae and cyanobacteria.
  • Some microorganisms use chemical energy instead of sunlight. This is called chemosynthesis.

2. Consumers (Heterotrophs) | వినియోగదారులు

  • Consumers obtain energy by feeding on other organisms or organic matter.
  • Primary consumers: Usually herbivores that eat producers. Examples: Deer, rabbit and grasshopper.
  • Secondary consumers: Feed on primary consumers. Examples: Frog eating grasshopper; small carnivorous fish eating zooplankton.
  • Tertiary consumers: Feed on secondary consumers. Example: Snake eating a frog.
  • Top consumers: Occupy high trophic positions in a particular food chain. Example: Eagle in some terrestrial food chains.
  • Omnivores: Feed on both plant and animal material. Example: Humans and bears.

3. Decomposers | వియోజకులు

  • Decomposers break down dead organisms and organic waste into simpler substances.
  • Important examples include bacteria and fungi.
  • They release nutrients back into the environment.
  • Decomposition helps maintain nutrient cycling.

4. Detritivores | శిథిల పదార్థ భక్షకులు

  • Detritivores feed on dead organic material or detritus.
  • Examples: Earthworms, millipedes and many soil-dwelling animals.
  • They fragment organic matter and support decomposition.
Important distinction: Detritivores ingest and fragment dead organic matter. Decomposers, such as many bacteria and fungi, chemically break it down.

3. Functions of an Ecosystem | పర్యావరణ వ్యవస్థ విధులు

An ecosystem performs several interconnected functions that maintain ecological processes.

Main functions

  1. Productivity: Formation of organic matter by producers and the accumulation of biomass.
  2. Decomposition: Breakdown of dead organic matter and release of nutrients.
  3. Energy flow: Transfer of energy through trophic levels.
  4. Nutrient cycling: Movement and reuse of essential nutrients between organisms and the environment.

Ecosystem services

Ecosystems also provide benefits that support human life and economic activity.

ServiceMeaningExamples
ProvisioningProducts obtained from ecosystemsFood, freshwater, timber, fibre and medicines.
RegulatingBenefits from regulation of natural processesClimate regulation, flood control, pollination and water purification.
CulturalNon-material benefitsRecreation, tourism, spiritual and educational values.
SupportingBasic processes that sustain other servicesNutrient cycling, soil formation and primary production.
Ecosystem services are the benefits people obtain from ecosystems. The four broad categories above are commonly used in the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment framework.

4. Productivity in Ecosystems | పర్యావరణ ఉత్పాదకత

Productivity refers to the rate at which biomass or organic matter is produced in an ecosystem over a given area and time.

A. Gross Primary Productivity (GPP)

Gross Primary Productivity is the total rate at which producers capture energy and convert it into organic matter through photosynthesis or other primary production processes.

ఉత్పాదకులు ఒక నిర్దిష్ట సమయంలో సృష్టించే మొత్తం సేంద్రీయ పదార్థం లేదా శక్తి ఉత్పత్తి రేటును Gross Primary Productivity అంటారు.

B. Net Primary Productivity (NPP)

Net Primary Productivity is the organic matter or energy remaining after producers use some of the captured energy for respiration.

Formula: NPP = GPP − R

Here, R = Respiration by producers.

C. Secondary Productivity

Secondary productivity is the rate at which consumers convert the food they assimilate into new biomass.

  • Primary productivity is associated with autotrophic producers.
  • Secondary productivity is associated with heterotrophic consumers.
  • NPP represents the biomass production available for plant growth, consumers and decomposers, subject to losses and ecological pathways.
Example: If GPP is 2,000 units and producer respiration is 800 units, then NPP = 2,000 − 800 = 1,200 units.

Productivity units

Productivity is commonly expressed as energy per unit area per unit time or dry mass per unit area per unit time, for example, kJ/m²/year or g/m²/year.

5. Food Chain | ఆహార గొలుసు

A food chain is a linear sequence of organisms through which food, nutrients and energy are transferred as one organism consumes another.

ఒక జీవి మరొక జీవిని ఆహారంగా తీసుకోవడం ద్వారా ఆహారం మరియు శక్తి ఒక జీవి నుంచి మరొక జీవికి క్రమంగా బదిలీ అయ్యే వరుసను ఆహార గొలుసు అంటారు.

A. Grazing Food Chain (GFC)

Begins with living green plants or other photosynthetic producers.

Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle

గడ్డి → మిడత → కప్ప → పాము → డేగ

B. Detritus Food Chain (DFC)

Begins with dead organic matter or detritus.

Dead leaves → Earthworm / Detritivore → Bird → Hawk

ఎండిన ఆకులు → వానపాము / శిథిల పదార్థ భక్షక జీవి → పక్షి → డేగ

C. Parasitic Food Chain

Energy passes from larger host organisms to smaller parasites and sometimes to hyperparasites.

Tree → Bird → Louse → Hyperparasite

ఈ ఉదాహరణలో పరాన్నజీవులు తమ ఆతిథ్య జీవులపై ఆధారపడతాయి.

Limitations of food chains

  • Many organisms consume more than one type of food.
  • A single organism may occupy different trophic levels in different food chains.
  • Food chains simplify complex feeding relationships.
  • Real ecosystems contain interconnected food chains.

6. Food Web | ఆహార జాలం

A food web is a network of interconnected food chains within an ecosystem.

ఒక పర్యావరణ వ్యవస్థలో అనేక ఆహార గొలుసులు పరస్పరం అనుసంధానమై ఏర్పడే సంక్లిష్ట సంబంధాల వ్యవస్థను ఆహార జాలం అంటారు.

Characteristics

  • An organism may have multiple food sources.
  • A consumer may be eaten by more than one predator.
  • Food webs represent feeding relationships more realistically than a single food chain.
  • Food webs can provide alternative energy pathways if one food source becomes scarce.
  • Changes in one population may affect several other populations.

Food chain vs Food web

Food ChainFood Web
Single linear feeding pathway.Network of interconnected feeding pathways.
Simplified representation.More complex representation of feeding relations.
Usually shows one route of energy transfer.Shows several possible routes.
Less representative of complex natural feeding relationships.Often represents ecosystem feeding relationships more realistically.

7. Trophic Levels | పోషక స్థాయులు

A trophic level is the feeding position occupied by an organism in a food chain.

Trophic LevelOrganismsExample
FirstProducersGrass, algae
SecondPrimary consumersGrasshopper, deer
ThirdSecondary consumersFrog, small carnivorous fish
FourthTertiary consumersSnake, larger fish
Higher levelsTop consumersEagle, tiger in particular food chains
The trophic level of an organism can vary depending on what it eats. Omnivores may occupy more than one trophic level.

Energy transfer

  • Energy enters most ecosystems through sunlight captured by photosynthetic producers.
  • Energy is transferred through feeding relationships.
  • At each transfer, a significant amount is used in metabolism and dissipated as heat.
  • Therefore, less energy is generally available at higher trophic levels.

8. Energy Flow in an Ecosystem | శక్తి ప్రవాహం

Energy flow is the transfer of energy from one trophic level to another through feeding relationships.

Important characteristics

  • Energy flow is unidirectional through trophic transfers.
  • Energy is not recycled in the same way nutrients are recycled.
  • Some energy is dissipated as heat during metabolic activities.
  • Most ecosystems receive energy primarily from sunlight.
  • Certain ecosystems, such as some deep-sea hydrothermal vent communities, are based on chemical energy rather than sunlight.

Lindeman's 10% Law

Raymond Lindeman proposed the trophic-dynamic concept in 1942. The commonly taught 10% law states that, on average, approximately 10% of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level.

ఒక పోషక స్థాయిలో ఉన్న శక్తిలో సగటున సుమారు 10 శాతం మాత్రమే తదుపరి పోషక స్థాయికి బదిలీ అవుతుందని 10% నియమం వివరిస్తుంది.

Illustrative example:
Producers: 10,000 units
Primary consumers: 1,000 units
Secondary consumers: 100 units
Tertiary consumers: 10 units

Important: The 10% value is a simplified ecological approximation, not an exact universal transfer rate for every ecosystem or food chain.

Why is energy lost?

  • Organisms use energy for respiration and metabolism.
  • Energy is used for movement and other life processes.
  • Not all consumed material is digested or assimilated.
  • Some energy leaves organisms as waste or remains in uneaten material.
  • Energy is ultimately dissipated as heat.

9. Ecological Pyramids | పర్యావరణ పిరమిడ్లు

Ecological pyramids are graphical representations of trophic relationships in terms of number, biomass or energy.

A. Pyramid of Numbers

Represents the number of individual organisms at each trophic level.

  • May be upright in a grassland ecosystem: many grasses support fewer herbivores and still fewer predators.
  • May be inverted or spindle-shaped in a tree ecosystem: one tree can support many insects and parasites.

B. Pyramid of Biomass

Represents the total biomass of organisms at each trophic level, usually measured as dry mass per unit area.

  • Usually upright in many terrestrial ecosystems.
  • Can be inverted in some aquatic ecosystems because phytoplankton have low standing biomass but rapid turnover.

C. Pyramid of Energy

Represents the rate of energy flow through trophic levels over a specified period.

  • Always upright when trophic levels and energy flow are represented consistently.
  • Energy available decreases at successive trophic levels.
  • It is generally considered the most informative pyramid for understanding energy transfer.
PyramidWhat it measuresCan be inverted?
NumbersNumber of organismsYes
BiomassTotal standing biomassYes
EnergyEnergy flow per unit area per unit timeNo, under consistent trophic accounting
Exam Point: Pyramid of energy is always upright. Pyramids of numbers and biomass may be upright or inverted depending on the ecosystem.

10. Ecological Efficiency | పర్యావరణ సామర్థ్యం

Ecological efficiency refers to the proportion of energy transferred from one trophic level to the next.

ఒక పోషక స్థాయిలో ఉన్న శక్తిలో తదుపరి స్థాయికి బదిలీ అయ్యే శక్తి శాతాన్ని పర్యావరణ సామర్థ్యం అంటారు.

Types of efficiency

  • Consumption efficiency: Proportion of available production at one level that is consumed by the next level.
  • Assimilation efficiency: Proportion of consumed energy that is absorbed rather than egested.
  • Production efficiency: Proportion of assimilated energy converted into new biomass.
  • Trophic transfer efficiency: Proportion of production at one trophic level transferred as production at the next level.

These efficiencies differ among organisms and ecosystems. The overall trophic transfer efficiency is influenced by feeding, digestion, metabolism and energy allocation.

11. Decomposition and Nutrient Recycling | వియోజనం మరియు పోషక పునర్వినియోగం

Decomposition is the breakdown of dead organic matter into simpler organic and inorganic substances.

Major steps of decomposition

  1. Fragmentation: Detritivores break dead material into smaller pieces.
  2. Leaching: Water dissolves and carries soluble substances into deeper soil layers.
  3. Catabolism: Microorganisms break complex organic substances into simpler compounds.
  4. Humification: Formation and accumulation of dark, relatively resistant organic matter called humus.
  5. Mineralisation: Release of inorganic nutrients from organic matter.

Factors affecting decomposition

  • Temperature: Warm conditions often increase microbial activity within suitable limits.
  • Moisture: Adequate moisture supports decomposer activity; extreme dryness or waterlogging may slow some processes.
  • Oxygen: Aerobic conditions generally support faster decomposition than oxygen-poor conditions for many organic materials.
  • Quality of detritus: Nitrogen-rich and easily decomposable material often breaks down faster than material rich in lignin or other resistant compounds.
  • Soil organisms: Microbes and detritivores influence decomposition rates.
Humus is relatively stable organic matter in soil. Mineralisation releases inorganic nutrients that can be reused by plants.

12. Ecosystem Balance and Stability | పర్యావరణ సమతుల్యత

Ecosystem stability refers to the ability of an ecosystem to maintain its structure and functions or recover after disturbances.

  • Resistance: Ability to withstand disturbance with limited change.
  • Resilience: Ability to recover after disturbance.
  • Natural regulation: Interactions such as predation, competition and resource limitation influence population sizes.
  • Biodiversity: Diversity of organisms can contribute to ecosystem functioning and resilience, although effects depend on ecosystem context.
  • Disturbance: Events such as fire, flood, drought, storms and human activities can change ecosystem structure.

Human impacts on ecosystems

  • Deforestation and habitat fragmentation.
  • Pollution of air, water and soil.
  • Overexploitation of natural resources.
  • Introduction and spread of invasive alien species.
  • Climate change and changes in rainfall and temperature patterns.
  • Land-use change and urbanisation.
Conservation, sustainable resource use, habitat restoration and pollution prevention help maintain ecosystem functions.
