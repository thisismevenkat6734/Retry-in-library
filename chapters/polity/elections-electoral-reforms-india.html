Overview | అవలోకనం

Elections are the foundation of representative democracy. They allow citizens to choose their representatives and hold governments accountable.

ప్రజాస్వామ్యంలో ఎన్నికలు అత్యంత ముఖ్యమైనవి. ప్రజలు తమ ప్రతినిధులను ఎన్నుకోవడానికి, ప్రభుత్వాలను జవాబుదారీగా ఉంచడానికి ఎన్నికలు ఉపయోగపడతాయి.

Exam Focus: Constitutional provisions, Representation of the People Acts, electoral systems, voter eligibility, EVM, VVPAT, NOTA, election disputes, electoral bonds judgment and electoral reforms.

Learning Objectives

  • Understand the constitutional framework of elections.
  • Study the First-Past-the-Post system.
  • Understand the role of the Election Commission.
  • Learn the election process from notification to results.
  • Understand EVM, VVPAT and NOTA.
  • Study important electoral laws and reforms.
  • Understand landmark Supreme Court judgments.

1. Constitutional Framework of Elections

The Constitution of India provides the basic framework for elections and representative government.

ArticleSubject
Article 324Superintendence, direction and control of elections vested in the Election Commission.
Article 325One general electoral roll; no exclusion from electoral rolls on grounds only of religion, race, caste or sex.
Article 326Elections to Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies on the basis of adult suffrage.
Article 327Power of Parliament to make laws relating to elections.
Article 328Power of State Legislatures to make laws relating to elections, subject to the Constitution and parliamentary law.
Article 329Bar to interference by courts in electoral matters except through the prescribed election petition process.
Article 243KState Election Commission for Panchayat elections.
Article 243ZAState Election Commission for Municipal elections.
Remember: Article 324 deals with the Election Commission of India. Articles 243K and 243ZA deal with local body elections through State Election Commissions.

2. Universal Adult Franchise | సార్వత్రిక వయోజన ఓటు హక్కు

Article 326 provides for elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies on the basis of adult suffrage.

  • Every Indian citizen who is at least 18 years old on the qualifying date and is not otherwise disqualified under the Constitution or applicable law is eligible for registration as an elector.
  • The voting age was reduced from 21 years to 18 years by the 61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1988.
  • The amendment came into effect in 1989.
  • Registration in the electoral roll is necessary to vote in an election.

Grounds of disqualification under Article 326

  • Non-residence.
  • Unsoundness of mind declared by a competent court.
  • Crime.
  • Corrupt or illegal practice.
  • Other grounds specified by law.
Being 18 years old does not automatically mean a person can vote without registration. The person's name must be included in the relevant electoral roll.

3. Electoral System in India

First-Past-the-Post System (FPTP)

India generally uses the First-Past-the-Post system for direct elections to the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.

  • The candidate who secures the highest number of valid votes in a constituency wins.
  • The winner does not necessarily need more than 50% of all votes cast.
  • Each constituency elects one representative.
  • It is also known as the simple plurality system.

Example

CandidateVotes
Candidate A40,000
Candidate B35,000
Candidate C25,000

Candidate A wins because A has the highest number of votes, even though A has not received more than 50% of the votes.

Proportional Representation

Under proportional representation, seats are allocated broadly in proportion to votes or through a preferential voting system, depending on the method used.

India uses proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote for certain indirect elections.

  • Election of the President.
  • Election of the Vice-President.
  • Election of Rajya Sabha members by elected members of State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Election of members of State Legislative Councils by specified electorates, where applicable.
Lok Sabha elections generally use FPTP. The President's election uses proportional representation by means of the single transferable vote and secret ballot.

4. Electoral Roll and Voter Registration

An electoral roll is the official list of eligible registered electors for a constituency or polling area.

Important features

  • Electoral rolls are prepared and revised under election laws.
  • Eligible citizens can apply for inclusion.
  • Corrections can be requested for errors in personal details.
  • Objections can be raised against wrongful inclusion or exclusion according to the law.
  • Electoral rolls may be revised periodically and through special revision processes.

Important forms

FormPurpose
Form 6Application for inclusion of name in electoral roll by an eligible elector.
Form 6AApplication for inclusion in electoral roll by an overseas elector.
Form 7Objection to proposed inclusion or request for deletion of an entry, as applicable.
Form 8Correction of entries, replacement of EPIC and certain other electoral roll-related services, including shifting of residence.

EPIC: Electors Photo Identity Card. It is commonly known as the Voter ID card.

A Voter ID card is an identity document, but the essential requirement to vote is that the elector's name appears on the electoral roll for that polling station or constituency.

5. Election Commission of India

The Election Commission of India is a constitutional body under Article 324.

Responsibilities

  • Conduct elections to Parliament.
  • Conduct elections to State Legislatures.
  • Conduct elections to the offices of President and Vice-President.
  • Supervise electoral rolls for elections within its jurisdiction.
  • Recognise political parties and allot symbols under applicable law and rules.
  • Monitor election expenditure and enforce election-related directions.
  • Issue the Model Code of Conduct when elections are announced.

Important distinction

The Election Commission of India does not conduct elections to Panchayats and Municipalities. These elections are conducted by State Election Commissions under Articles 243K and 243ZA.

6. Election Laws in India

Representation of the People Act, 1950

  • Allocation of seats in the House of the People and State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Delimitation-related provisions within its statutory framework.
  • Electoral rolls and voter registration.
  • Qualifications of voters and related matters.

Representation of the People Act, 1951

  • Conduct of elections.
  • Qualifications and disqualifications of candidates.
  • Election offences and corrupt practices.
  • Election disputes and election petitions.
  • Political party registration under Section 29A.
  • Regulation of election expenditure and other election-related matters.
RPA 1950 mainly deals with electoral rolls and allocation-related matters. RPA 1951 mainly deals with the conduct of elections, qualifications, disqualifications and disputes.

7. Election Process in India

  1. Announcement: Election schedule is announced by the Election Commission.
  2. Model Code of Conduct: It comes into operation from the announcement of the election schedule.
  3. Notification: Formal notification is issued under the applicable law.
  4. Nomination: Candidates file nomination papers.
  5. Scrutiny: Returning Officer examines nomination papers.
  6. Withdrawal: Candidates may withdraw within the prescribed period.
  7. Campaigning: Candidates and parties campaign according to election laws and the Model Code of Conduct.
  8. Polling: Eligible electors cast their votes.
  9. Counting: Votes are counted under prescribed procedures.
  10. Declaration: Results are declared by the Returning Officer.

Important election officers

  • Chief Electoral Officer: Supervises election work at State or Union Territory level under the Election Commission.
  • District Election Officer: Coordinates election administration in a district.
  • Returning Officer: Responsible for conducting the election in a constituency according to law.
  • Presiding Officer: Responsible for conducting polling at a polling station.
  • Booth Level Officer: Assists in electoral roll-related work at the local level.

8. Electronic Voting Machine (EVM)

An Electronic Voting Machine is used to record votes electronically in elections where the Election Commission adopts the system.

Main components

  • Ballot Unit: Displays candidate names and symbols; the voter presses the button against the chosen candidate.
  • Control Unit: Operated by polling officials and used to control voting and store recorded votes.
  • VVPAT: Provides a paper audit trail showing the choice recorded by the voter.

Advantages

  • Faster counting compared with traditional manual counting.
  • Reduces invalid votes caused by improperly marked ballot papers.
  • Designed to prevent multiple votes by one elector in the same poll.
  • Helps streamline election administration.
EVMs are subject to security procedures, sealing, mock polls, candidate and agent participation, and prescribed verification processes. Public trust depends on transparency and effective safeguards.

9. VVPAT – Voter Verifiable Paper Audit Trail

VVPAT is a system connected to the EVM that allows a voter to visually verify the choice recorded by the machine.

How it works

  1. The voter presses the button for the selected candidate on the Ballot Unit.
  2. The VVPAT printer produces a paper slip showing the candidate's serial number, name and symbol.
  3. The slip is visible through a transparent window for approximately seven seconds.
  4. The slip then falls into a sealed drop box.

Importance

  • Provides a voter-verifiable paper trail.
  • Improves transparency and confidence in the voting process.
  • Supports prescribed verification and audit procedures.

VVPAT was introduced in Indian elections on a trial basis in 2013. It was first used in a by-election in Nagaland.

The Supreme Court's 2024 judgment in Association for Democratic Reforms v. Election Commission of India rejected a request for 100% VVPAT slip counting but directed additional safeguards, including preservation of certain symbol-loading units and a post-result verification option for specified candidates under prescribed conditions.

10. NOTA – None of the Above

NOTA allows an elector to indicate that they do not wish to vote for any of the listed candidates.

  • Introduced in Indian elections following the Supreme Court judgment in People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2013).
  • Available as an option on EVMs.
  • It protects the secrecy of an elector's choice.
  • NOTA does not currently operate as a right to automatically cancel an election if it receives the highest number of votes in Lok Sabha or State Assembly elections.
  • The candidate with the highest valid votes among the candidates can still win under the applicable election system, even if NOTA receives more votes.
NOTA is not the same as a negative vote that automatically defeats all candidates or requires a fresh election in general Lok Sabha and Assembly elections.

11. Model Code of Conduct (MCC)

The Model Code of Conduct is a set of guidelines issued by the Election Commission to regulate the conduct of political parties and candidates during elections.

Major principles

  • Political parties should avoid activities that create hatred or tension between communities.
  • Criticism should generally be based on policies, programmes and public record rather than personal attacks.
  • Religious or communal appeals for votes are prohibited under applicable election laws and MCC provisions.
  • Ministers should not use official machinery for election campaigning.
  • Government resources should not be misused to influence voters.
  • Public meetings and processions must follow applicable permissions and restrictions.
  • Parties should cooperate with election authorities and follow campaign rules.

When does MCC apply?

It comes into force when the Election Commission announces the election schedule. It remains applicable until the election process is completed, subject to the relevant directions and legal framework.

The MCC is not a separate Act of Parliament. Some of its provisions are enforceable through existing laws, while other provisions operate as directions and guidelines of the Election Commission.

12. Election Expenditure

Election expenditure rules aim to promote transparency and reduce the influence of excessive money in elections.

  • Recognised and other candidates must maintain election expenditure accounts as required by law.
  • Accounts must be lodged within the prescribed time after the election result.
  • Failure to lodge accounts without a sufficient reason can result in disqualification under the Representation of the People Act, 1951.
  • Expenditure limits are prescribed by the Election Commission under the relevant legal framework and government notifications.
  • Political parties have separate reporting and disclosure obligations under applicable laws.
Candidate expenditure limits and political party expenditure are not identical. Always distinguish between a candidate's legal expenditure account and a party's overall election spending.

13. Political Parties and Recognition

Political parties are registered with the Election Commission under Section 29A of the Representation of the People Act, 1951.

Recognition

The Election Symbols (Reservation and Allotment) Order, 1968 provides the framework for recognition of political parties and reservation or allotment of election symbols.

  • National parties satisfy specified performance criteria in elections across States.
  • State parties satisfy specified performance criteria within a State.
  • Recognition provides certain electoral privileges, including reserved symbols and other benefits under applicable rules.

National party recognition criteria

A party may qualify through any one of the prescribed routes under the Symbols Order, including:

  • Securing at least 6% of valid votes in four or more States in the relevant Lok Sabha or Assembly elections and winning at least four Lok Sabha seats.
  • Winning at least 2% of Lok Sabha seats, with candidates elected from at least three States.
  • Being recognised as a State party in at least four States.

State party recognition

A party may qualify through any one of the prescribed performance routes, including vote-share and seat-share criteria in the relevant State elections. The exact criteria are governed by the Election Symbols Order.

14. Delimitation of Constituencies

Delimitation means the process of fixing or redrawing the boundaries of territorial constituencies for elections.

Objectives

  • Ensure broadly fair representation of population.
  • Adjust constituency boundaries after changes in population.
  • Support the constitutional principle of equal representation as far as practicable.
  • Determine reserved constituencies for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes according to constitutional and legal provisions.

Delimitation Commission

  • It is established under a law made by Parliament.
  • It carries out delimitation according to the law and the relevant constitutional framework.
  • Its orders have the force of law and are generally not open to challenge in court, subject to the constitutional framework.

Constitutional background

The 42nd Constitutional Amendment froze the readjustment of seats based on population until after the first census conducted after 2000. The 84th Amendment extended the freeze on inter-State allocation of Lok Sabha and Assembly seats until the first census after 2026, while allowing certain readjustments of constituencies within States. The 87th Amendment permitted delimitation based on the 2001 Census without changing the number of seats allocated to States.

The constitutional position is more specific than a simple statement that all delimitation is frozen until 2026. Distinguish inter-State seat allocation from internal constituency readjustment.

15. Disqualification of Candidates and Members

The Constitution and election laws prescribe qualifications and disqualifications for membership of Parliament and State Legislatures.

Article 102 – Disqualification for Parliament

  • Holding an office of profit under the Union or State Government, subject to exceptions made by Parliament.
  • Being of unsound mind declared by a competent court.
  • Being an undischarged insolvent.
  • Not being a citizen of India, or voluntarily acquiring citizenship of a foreign State, or acknowledging allegiance to a foreign State.
  • Disqualification under any law made by Parliament.

Article 191 – Disqualification for State Legislature

Article 191 contains corresponding grounds for disqualification of members of State Legislatures.

Section 8 of RPA, 1951

Provides for disqualification on conviction for specified offences, subject to statutory provisions.

The Representation of the People Act, 1951 contains detailed provisions concerning qualifications, disqualifications and election disputes.

16. Anti-Defection and Elections

The Tenth Schedule of the Constitution deals with disqualification on the ground of defection.

  • Added by the 52nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1985.
  • Applies to members of Parliament and State Legislatures.
  • Voluntarily giving up membership of a political party can attract disqualification.
  • Voting or abstaining contrary to a party direction, without permission or condonation within the prescribed period, can attract disqualification.
  • Independent members joining a political party after election can attract disqualification.
  • Nominated members joining a political party after the permitted six-month period can attract disqualification.

Merger exception

The merger exception applies when at least two-thirds of the members of the legislature party agree to the merger, subject to the Tenth Schedule.

Decision-making authority

The Speaker or Chairman decides disqualification petitions under the Tenth Schedule, subject to judicial review.

17. Criminalisation of Politics

Criminalisation of politics refers to the increasing involvement of persons facing serious criminal allegations or convictions in electoral politics and legislative institutions.

Major concerns

  • Use of money and muscle power.
  • Threats to free and fair elections.
  • Reduced public confidence in democratic institutions.
  • Influence of organised crime and corruption.
  • Delayed criminal trials.

Existing legal framework

  • Candidates are required to disclose criminal antecedents as prescribed by law and judicial directions.
  • Conviction for specified offences can lead to disqualification under Section 8 of the RPA, 1951.
  • Political parties have disclosure and publicity obligations concerning candidates with criminal cases under Supreme Court directions.
  • Election affidavits provide information about assets, liabilities, educational qualifications and criminal cases.

18. Important Supreme Court Judgments

1. Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975)

The Supreme Court considered the relationship between free and fair elections and the basic structure of the Constitution. Free and fair elections are recognised as an important feature of democracy.

2. Mohinder Singh Gill v. Chief Election Commissioner (1978)

The Court explained the broad scope of Article 324 and the Election Commission's powers where the law does not expressly provide for a particular situation, subject to the Constitution and laws.

3. Union of India v. Association for Democratic Reforms (2002)

The Supreme Court recognised the voter's right to know important information about candidates, including criminal antecedents, assets, liabilities and educational qualifications.

4. People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2003)

The Court upheld the importance of voters' right to information about candidates and addressed the statutory framework restricting disclosure.

5. People's Union for Civil Liberties v. Union of India (2013)

The Supreme Court directed the introduction of the NOTA option in voting machines and ballot papers, protecting the secrecy of voters who choose not to vote for any candidate.

6. Lily Thomas v. Union of India (2013)

The Supreme Court struck down the protection previously available under Section 8(4) of the RPA, 1951 to sitting legislators against immediate disqualification after conviction in specified circumstances.

7. Public Interest Foundation v. Union of India (2018)

The Court issued directions for political parties and candidates to publicise criminal antecedents in the manner prescribed.

8. Association for Democratic Reforms v. Union of India (2024)

The Supreme Court struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme as unconstitutional, holding that the scheme violated the right to information under Article 19(1)(a), among other findings.

9. Association for Democratic Reforms v. Election Commission of India (2024)

The Supreme Court rejected a request for 100% VVPAT slip counting but directed additional safeguards, including a limited post-result verification process for specified candidates under prescribed conditions.

19. Electoral Bonds Judgment and Political Funding

Electoral bonds were introduced as a mechanism for political contributions through eligible banking channels. The Supreme Court struck down the Electoral Bonds Scheme in February 2024.

Important points

  • The Supreme Court held that the scheme violated the voters' right to information under Article 19(1)(a).
  • The Court directed the State Bank of India to stop issuing electoral bonds.
  • The Court directed disclosure of relevant bond information through the Election Commission.
  • The judgment highlighted the importance of transparency in political funding.
Electoral bonds are no longer a valid ongoing political contribution scheme following the Supreme Court's 2024 judgment.

20. Electoral Reforms in India

Major reform areas

  • Greater transparency in political funding.
  • Disclosure of candidates' criminal antecedents.
  • Effective monitoring of election expenditure.
  • Strengthening the independence and capacity of election authorities.
  • Improving voter registration and accessibility.
  • Reducing money power and muscle power.
  • Improving transparency in political party accounts.
  • Ensuring accessible polling facilities for persons with disabilities and senior citizens.
  • Improving the security and auditability of electronic voting systems.
  • Promoting voter awareness and participation.

Important committees and recommendations

Committee / CommissionMajor focus
Tarkunde Committee (1975–1976)Electoral reforms and democratic election processes.
Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990)Electoral reforms, election expenses and election administration.
Indrajit Gupta Committee (1998)State funding of elections.
Law Commission of India, 170th Report (1999)Reform of electoral laws and political party regulation.
National Commission to Review the Working of the Constitution (2000–2002)Constitutional and electoral reforms.
Second Administrative Reforms CommissionEthics in governance and electoral accountability.

Possible reform measures

  • Strengthen transparency in political donations.
  • Ensure faster trials in criminal cases involving elected representatives, with safeguards against misuse.
  • Improve internal democracy and financial accountability of political parties.
  • Strengthen voter education.
  • Review election expenditure rules and enforcement mechanisms.
  • Improve accessibility and inclusiveness in polling.

22. Mains Answer Framework

Question: Discuss the major challenges to free and fair elections in India and suggest reforms.

Introduction: Free and fair elections are essential for representative democracy and are recognised as an important feature of the basic structure of the Constitution.

Challenges:

  • Money power and opaque political funding.
  • Criminalisation of politics.
  • Misuse of official machinery and hate speech.
  • Paid news and misinformation.
  • Unequal campaign resources.
  • Concerns about voter access and electoral roll accuracy.

Reforms:

  • Improve transparency in political funding.
  • Strengthen election expenditure monitoring.
  • Ensure timely disclosure of candidate information.
  • Improve electoral roll accuracy.
  • Strengthen voter awareness and accessibility.
  • Provide adequate institutional resources and independence to election authorities.

Conclusion: Electoral reforms must strengthen transparency, accountability, voter participation and institutional trust to protect India's democratic system.
