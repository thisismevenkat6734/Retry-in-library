1. Introduction – పరిచయం

A tribunal is a quasi-judicial body established by law to decide disputes in specialised areas. Tribunals generally provide specialised adjudication outside the ordinary court structure, subject to constitutional judicial review.

ట్రిబ్యునల్ అనేది నిర్దిష్ట రంగాల్లో వివాదాలను పరిష్కరించడానికి చట్టం ద్వారా ఏర్పాటు చేసే అర్ధ-న్యాయ సంస్థ. ఇవి ప్రత్యేక అంశాలపై న్యాయపరమైన నిర్ణయాలు తీసుకుంటాయి.

Why are Tribunals established?

  • Specialised knowledge in technical and administrative matters.
  • Speedier resolution of specified disputes.
  • Reduction of the burden on ordinary courts.
  • Flexible procedures, subject to governing laws and principles of natural justice.

Exam Point: Part XIV-A = Articles 323A and 323B

3. Article 323A – Administrative Tribunals

Article 323A empowers Parliament to provide by law for adjudication or trial by administrative tribunals of disputes and complaints concerning recruitment and conditions of service of persons appointed to public services and posts in connection with the affairs of the Union, States, local authorities and other specified public bodies.

కేంద్ర, రాష్ట్ర ప్రభుత్వాలు మరియు నిర్దిష్ట ప్రభుత్వ సంస్థల్లో ఉద్యోగ నియామకాలు, సేవా నిబంధనలకు సంబంధించిన వివాదాల పరిష్కారం కోసం పార్లమెంట్ చట్టం ద్వారా Administrative Tribunals ఏర్పాటు చేయవచ్చు.

Important features

  • Only Parliament can make a law under Article 323A.
  • Parliament may establish one Administrative Tribunal for the Union and a separate tribunal for a State or group of States.
  • Administrative Tribunals deal with recruitment and conditions of service disputes.
  • They function according to the governing law and applicable procedural principles.

Central Administrative Tribunal (CAT)

The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 was enacted under Article 323A. The Central Administrative Tribunal was established on 1 November 1985.

  • It adjudicates covered service matters concerning Union public services and posts, subject to statutory jurisdiction.
  • It also deals with covered matters of specified public authorities and organisations under the Act.
  • Its principal seat is at New Delhi, with benches in different locations.
  • Its jurisdiction is governed by the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 and subsequent legal developments.

State Administrative Tribunals (SATs)

Under the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985, a State Administrative Tribunal may be established for a State on the request of the concerned State Government, subject to the statutory process. A Joint Administrative Tribunal may also be established for two or more States.

రాష్ట్ర ప్రభుత్వ అభ్యర్థన మరియు చట్టపరమైన ప్రక్రియ ఆధారంగా రాష్ట్ర పరిపాలనా ట్రిబ్యునల్ ఏర్పాటు చేయవచ్చు.

4. Article 323B – Tribunals for Other Matters

Article 323B empowers the appropriate Legislature to provide by law for tribunals dealing with specified subjects.

Article 323B ప్రకారం సంబంధిత శాసనసభ నిర్దిష్ట అంశాలపై ట్రిబ్యునల్స్ ఏర్పాటు చేయడానికి చట్టం చేయవచ్చు.

Subjects under Article 323B

  1. Taxation.
  2. Foreign exchange, import and export.
  3. Industrial and labour disputes.
  4. Land reforms and ceiling on urban property.
  5. Ceiling on agricultural land.
  6. Election disputes to Parliament and State Legislatures, subject to constitutional provisions.
  7. Production, procurement, supply and distribution of foodstuffs and other essential goods.
  8. Rent, tenancy and related matters.

Important features

  • Both Parliament and the appropriate State Legislature may legislate within their respective legislative competence.
  • It covers the subjects specifically mentioned in Article 323B.
  • It is broader in subject matter than Article 323A.

Remember: Article 323A = Administrative service matters | Article 323B = Specified other matters

7. Important Supreme Court Judgments

1. S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (1987)

The Supreme Court considered the constitutional validity of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 and the role of tribunals as an effective alternative institutional mechanism for adjudication of service disputes.

2. L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India (1997)

This is a landmark judgment on judicial review and tribunals.

  • The power of judicial review of High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 and of the Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the Constitution's basic structure.
  • Tribunals can act as courts of first instance in matters within their jurisdiction.
  • Decisions of tribunals are subject to scrutiny by the jurisdictional High Courts under Articles 226 and 227.
  • Tribunals cannot completely replace the constitutional judicial review powers of High Courts and the Supreme Court.

L. Chandra Kumar కేసు ప్రకారం హైకోర్టుల న్యాయ సమీక్షాధికారం రాజ్యాంగ మౌలిక నిర్మాణంలో భాగం. ట్రిబ్యునల్స్ తీర్పులు సంబంధిత హైకోర్టుల సమీక్షకు లోబడి ఉంటాయి.

3. Union of India v. R. Gandhi (2010)

The Supreme Court examined the constitutionality and institutional design of the National Company Law Tribunal and National Company Law Appellate Tribunal, emphasising judicial independence and appropriate qualifications for members.

4. Madras Bar Association Cases

In a series of judgments, the Supreme Court examined tribunal independence, appointments, tenure, qualifications, administration and the role of the executive in tribunal governance.

8. Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021

The Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 made changes concerning specified tribunals, including provisions on appointments, qualifications, tenure and service conditions.

  • It addressed the structure and functioning of certain tribunals.
  • It included provisions on search-cum-selection committees and appointment processes.
  • Several provisions of the 2021 framework were examined by the Supreme Court in tribunal-related litigation.
  • For current recruitment and tribunal administration, the applicable statutory text and subsequent judicial decisions must be checked.

Tribunal Reforms Act, 2021 ద్వారా కొన్ని ట్రిబ్యునల్స్ నియామకాలు, అర్హతలు, పదవీకాలం మరియు పరిపాలనలో మార్పులు చేశారు. అయితే దాని కొన్ని నిబంధనలను సుప్రీంకోర్టు పరిశీలించింది.

9. Advantages and Challenges

Advantages

  • Specialised knowledge and expertise.
  • Potentially quicker dispute resolution.
  • Reduced pressure on ordinary courts.
  • Flexible procedures.

Challenges

  • Vacancies and delays.
  • Questions about independence from the executive.
  • Infrastructure and staffing shortages.
  • Overlapping jurisdiction and procedural complexity.
  • Need for effective judicial review and accountability.
