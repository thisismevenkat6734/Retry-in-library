1. Introduction | పరిచయం

భారతదేశంలో కేంద్ర ప్రభుత్వం మరియు రాష్ట్ర ప్రభుత్వాల మధ్య అధికారాలు, బాధ్యతలు, వనరులు ఎలా పంచబడతాయో వివరించే వ్యవస్థను Centre–State Relations అంటారు.

Centre–State Relations are the constitutional arrangements governing the distribution of legislative, administrative and financial powers between the Union and the States.

Part XI Articles 245–263 Part XII Articles 268–293 Seventh Schedule

Three Major Dimensions

  1. Legislative Relations – శాసన సంబంధాలు.
  2. Administrative Relations – పరిపాలనా సంబంధాలు.
  3. Financial Relations – ఆర్థిక సంబంధాలు.
Exam Point: Legislative and Administrative Relations are mainly covered under Part XI. Financial Relations are mainly covered under Part XII.

2. Indian Federalism | భారత సమాఖ్య వ్యవస్థ

India has a federal constitutional structure with a comparatively strong Union. The Constitution distributes powers between the Union and States, while also providing mechanisms for national coordination and unity.

Federal Features – సమాఖ్య లక్షణాలు

  • Written Constitution – లిఖిత రాజ్యాంగం.
  • Supremacy of Constitution – రాజ్యాంగ ఆధిపత్యం.
  • Division of powers – అధికారాల విభజన.
  • Independent Judiciary – స్వతంత్ర న్యాయవ్యవస్థ.
  • Partly rigid Constitution – కొంత కఠినమైన రాజ్యాంగ సవరణ విధానం.
  • Bicameral Parliament – కేంద్రంలో రెండు సభలు.

Unitary Features – ఏకకేంద్ర లక్షణాలు

  • Strong Union Government.
  • Single citizenship.
  • Integrated judiciary.
  • Emergency provisions.
  • Residuary powers with Parliament.
  • All India Services.
  • Governors appointed by the President.
Important: India is often described as a federation with a strong Centre. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar explained that the Constitution can work as federal in normal times and may become more unitary in certain emergency circumstances.

Article 1 – India, that is Bharat

Article 1 describes India as a Union of States. The expression "Union of States" indicates that the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among independent States, and States do not have a constitutional right to secede.

3. Legislative Relations | శాసన సంబంధాలు

Part XI, Chapter I – Articles 245–255 deal with legislative relations between the Union and States.

Article 245 – Extent of Laws

  • Parliament may make laws for the whole or any part of the territory of India.
  • State Legislature may make laws for the whole or any part of the State.
  • Parliament's law is not invalid merely because it has extra-territorial operation.

Article 246 – Distribution of Legislative Subjects

Article 246 read with the Seventh Schedule distributes legislative subjects among three lists.

ListLegislative AuthorityExamples
Union ListParliamentDefence, Foreign Affairs, Railways, Currency
State ListState LegislaturesPolice, Public Order, Agriculture, Public Health
Concurrent ListBoth Parliament and State LegislaturesEducation, Forests, Marriage, Criminal Law
Important: Education, Forests, Protection of Wild Animals and Birds, Weights and Measures, and Administration of Justice were among the subjects shifted from the State List to the Concurrent List by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976.

Seventh Schedule – Quick Facts

  • Union List – originally 97 entries.
  • State List – originally 66 entries.
  • Concurrent List – originally 47 entries.
  • These are original constitutional counts; amendments have changed the lists and entries over time.

Article 248 – Residuary Powers

Parliament has exclusive power to make laws on matters not enumerated in the State List or Concurrent List, including residuary taxation, subject to the Constitution.

Article 249 – National Interest

Rajya Sabha may pass a resolution, supported by not less than two-thirds of members present and voting, declaring that it is necessary or expedient in the national interest for Parliament to legislate on a matter in the State List.

  • The resolution remains in force for a period not exceeding one year.
  • It may be renewed for further periods of one year at a time.
  • A law made under this Article ceases to have effect, to the extent provided by the Constitution, six months after the resolution ceases to operate, subject to things already done or omitted to be done.

Article 250 – During National Emergency

While a Proclamation of Emergency is in operation, Parliament can legislate on matters in the State List.

Such a law ceases to have effect, to the extent provided by the Constitution, six months after the Emergency ceases to operate, subject to things already done or omitted to be done.

Article 252 – Consent of Two or More States

When two or more State Legislatures pass resolutions requesting Parliament to legislate on a State List matter, Parliament may make a law for those States.

  • Other States may adopt that law later by passing resolutions.
  • Only Parliament can amend or repeal a law made under Article 252.

Article 253 – International Agreements

Parliament has power to make laws for implementing treaties, agreements and conventions with foreign countries or decisions made at international conferences, even on subjects otherwise within the State List.

Article 254 – Repugnancy

When a State law and a Parliamentary law conflict on a Concurrent List subject, the Parliamentary law generally prevails.

  • To the extent of repugnancy, the State law is void unless the constitutional exception applies.
  • If a State law reserved for the President receives Presidential assent, it may prevail in that State.
  • Parliament can subsequently override or amend that State law.

Article 246A – GST Legislative Power

  • Parliament and State Legislatures have power to make laws with respect to GST, subject to Article 246A.
  • Parliament has exclusive power to make laws with respect to GST on inter-State trade or commerce.
  • Article 269A deals with levy and collection of GST in the course of inter-State trade or commerce.

4. Administrative Relations | పరిపాలనా సంబంధాలు

Administrative Relations between the Union and States are mainly covered by Articles 256–263 in Part XI, Chapter II.

Article 256 – Obligation of States

States must exercise their executive power in a manner that ensures compliance with laws made by Parliament and existing laws applicable in the State.

The Union may give directions to a State as provided by Article 256.

Article 257 – Control of the Union over States

State executive power must be exercised so as not to impede or prejudice the exercise of Union executive power. The Union may give directions in the circumstances specified by Article 257.

Article 258 – Union Entrusting Functions to States

The President may, with the consent of the State Government, entrust Union executive functions to the State or its officers, subject to constitutional provisions.

Article 258A – States Entrusting Functions to Union

The Governor of a State may, with the consent of the Government of India, entrust State executive functions to the Union or its officers.

Article 262 – Inter-State Water Disputes

Parliament may provide by law for adjudication of disputes relating to the use, distribution or control of waters of inter-State rivers or river valleys.

Parliament may also provide that neither the Supreme Court nor any other court shall exercise jurisdiction over such disputes, subject to the constitutional and statutory framework.

Article 263 – Inter-State Council

The President may establish an Inter-State Council if it appears that public interests would be served by its establishment.

Its functions may include:

  • Inquiring into and advising upon disputes between States.
  • Investigating and discussing subjects of common interest.
  • Making recommendations for better coordination of policy and action.

5. Financial Relations | ఆర్థిక సంబంధాలు

Financial Relations between the Union and States are mainly dealt with in Part XII, especially Articles 268–293.

Tax Distribution

The Constitution provides different arrangements for the levy, collection and distribution of taxes between the Union and States.

ArticleSubject
268Duties levied by Union but collected and appropriated by States in specified cases
269Taxes levied and collected by Union but assigned to States, as constitutionally specified
269AGST on inter-State trade or commerce
270Taxes levied and distributed between Union and States
271Union surcharge on certain duties and taxes
275Grants-in-aid from Union to certain States
280Finance Commission
282Grants for any public purpose

Article 270 – Distribution of Taxes

Article 270 provides for distribution between the Union and States of specified Union taxes and duties, subject to constitutional exceptions.

Article 271 – Surcharge

Parliament may increase certain duties and taxes by a surcharge for Union purposes. The surcharge is not part of the divisible pool under Article 270.

Article 275 – Grants-in-Aid

Parliament may provide grants-in-aid from the Consolidated Fund of India to States in need of assistance, including grants for specified purposes.

Article 282 – Discretionary Grants

The Union or a State may make grants for any public purpose, even if the purpose is not within its legislative competence, subject to constitutional provisions.

Article 293 – State Borrowing

  • States may borrow within India upon the security of their Consolidated Funds, subject to constitutional limits.
  • When a State has outstanding loans or guarantees from the Union, further borrowing may require Union consent under Article 293(3).
  • Consent may be subject to conditions under Article 293(4).

6. Important Institutions for Centre–State Relations

A. Finance Commission – Article 280

Finance Commission is a constitutional body established by the President.

  • Constituted normally every five years or earlier if necessary.
  • Consists of a Chairman and four other members.
  • Recommends distribution of net proceeds of taxes between Union and States.
  • Recommends allocation among States of their respective shares.
  • Recommends principles governing grants-in-aid.
  • May recommend measures to augment State funds for Panchayats and Municipalities based on State Finance Commission recommendations.
Remember: Finance Commission is a Constitutional Body under Article 280.

B. GST Council – Article 279A

GST Council is a constitutional body established under Article 279A.

Composition:

  • Union Finance Minister – Chairperson.
  • Union Minister of State in charge of Revenue or Finance – Member.
  • Minister in charge of Finance or Taxation, or another nominated Minister, from each State – Members.

Important functions:

  • Recommendations on GST rates and exemptions.
  • Recommendations on model GST laws.
  • Recommendations on special provisions for certain States.
  • Recommendations on threshold limits and other GST-related matters.

Voting:

  • Quorum: one-half of the total membership.
  • Union Government vote weight: one-third of total votes cast.
  • States collectively: two-thirds of total votes cast.
  • A decision requires at least three-fourths of weighted votes of members present and voting.

C. Inter-State Council – Article 263

Inter-State Council is intended to improve coordination between the Union and States and among States.

The Inter-State Council was established in 1990 by a Presidential Order on the recommendation of the Sarkaria Commission.

D. Zonal Councils

Zonal Councils were established under the States Reorganisation Act, 1956. They are statutory bodies, not constitutional bodies.

There are five Zonal Councils:

  1. Northern Zonal Council
  2. Central Zonal Council
  3. Eastern Zonal Council
  4. Western Zonal Council
  5. Southern Zonal Council

The Union Home Minister is the common Chairman of the five Zonal Councils. The Chief Ministers of the member States serve as Vice-Chairpersons by rotation, as provided by the Act.

North Eastern Council

The North Eastern Council was established under the North Eastern Council Act, 1971. It is a statutory body and is distinct from the five Zonal Councils.

E. NITI Aayog

NITI Aayog is an executive policy think tank established by a Union Government resolution in 2015. It promotes cooperative federalism and competitive federalism. It is not a constitutional body.

7. Important Commissions

Sarkaria Commission (1983)

Set up to examine Centre–State relations and recommend improvements.

  • Emphasised cooperative federalism.
  • Recommended that Article 356 should be used sparingly.
  • Discussed the role of Governors and the need for appropriate consultation.
  • Recommended strengthening the Inter-State Council.

Punchhi Commission (2007)

Set up to examine Centre–State relations in the changing political and economic context.

  • Examined the role and appointment of Governors.
  • Considered the use of Article 356.
  • Discussed issues relating to internal security and federal coordination.
  • Made recommendations on improving Centre–State cooperation.

Rajamannar Committee (1969)

Appointed by the Tamil Nadu Government to examine Centre–State relations.

  • Called for greater State autonomy.
  • Examined the distribution of powers.
  • Recommended institutional changes in Centre–State relations.

8. Cooperative and Competitive Federalism

Cooperative Federalism

కేంద్రం మరియు రాష్ట్రాలు పరస్పర సహకారంతో దేశ అభివృద్ధి కోసం పనిచేయడం Cooperative Federalism.

Examples:

  • GST Council.
  • Inter-State Council.
  • Finance Commission.
  • Joint policy implementation.
  • Central and State cooperation in disaster management.

Competitive Federalism

రాష్ట్రాలు పెట్టుబడులు, మౌలిక సదుపాయాలు, పరిపాలనా పనితీరు, అభివృద్ధి సూచికల విషయంలో పరస్పరం పోటీ పడే విధానాన్ని Competitive Federalism అంటారు.

Examples:

  • Investment attraction.
  • Ease of doing business reforms.
  • Infrastructure development.
  • Performance-based rankings and policy comparisons.
Exam Difference: Cooperative Federalism focuses on collaboration; Competitive Federalism focuses on performance-based competition among States.

10. Mains Answer Writing Framework

Question: Explain the legislative relations between the Union and States.

Introduction: Legislative relations are mainly governed by Part XI, Articles 245–255, and the Seventh Schedule.

Distribution of powers: Explain Union List, State List and Concurrent List under Article 246.

Parliament's special powers: Discuss Articles 249, 250, 252 and 253.

Repugnancy: Explain Article 254 and the role of Presidential assent.

Conclusion: The system balances State autonomy with national unity and legislative coordination.

Question: Discuss the importance of cooperative federalism in India.

Introduction: Cooperative federalism involves collaboration between the Union and States in policy-making and implementation.

Institutions: Discuss GST Council, Finance Commission, Inter-State Council and Zonal Councils.

Benefits: Better coordination, resource sharing, policy consistency and regional development.

Challenges: Fiscal imbalance, political differences, disputes over resources and overlapping responsibilities.

Conclusion: Strong institutional dialogue and mutual trust are essential for effective federal governance.
