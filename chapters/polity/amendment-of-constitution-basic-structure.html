Chapter Overview | అధ్యాయ పరిచయం

The Constitution of India is neither completely rigid nor completely flexible. It provides a special procedure for constitutional amendments so that the Constitution can adapt to changing social, political and economic conditions while preserving its essential identity.

1. Meaning and Need for Constitutional Amendment

What is a Constitutional Amendment?

A Constitutional Amendment is a formal change made to the Constitution through the procedure prescribed by the Constitution itself.

తెలుగు: రాజ్యాంగంలో కొత్త నిబంధన చేర్చడం, ఉన్న నిబంధనను మార్చడం లేదా తొలగించడం వంటి మార్పులను రాజ్యాంగ సవరణ అంటారు.

Why are amendments necessary?

  • Social change: Society and social priorities change over time.
  • Economic development: New economic policies and institutions may require constitutional changes.
  • Political reforms: Changes may be required in governance and democratic institutions.
  • Administrative needs: New States, institutions and constitutional arrangements may be necessary.
  • Correction: Amendments can address practical difficulties and gaps in constitutional provisions.

Nature of the Indian Constitution

The Indian Constitution combines flexibility with rigidity.

Remember: Some provisions can be changed by a simple majority of Parliament, some require a special majority, and certain federal provisions require both a special parliamentary majority and ratification by at least half of the State Legislatures.

2. Methods of Constitutional Change

For examination purposes, constitutional changes are commonly classified into three broad methods.

Method 1 – Simple Majority of Parliament

Some provisions can be changed by a simple majority of members present and voting in each House. Such changes are generally not treated as amendments under Article 368.

Examples include certain matters relating to:

  • Formation of new States and alteration of areas, boundaries or names of States under Articles 2, 3 and 4.
  • Creation or abolition of a State Legislative Council under Article 169.
  • Matters relating to citizenship under Article 11.
  • Changes concerning certain parliamentary and administrative matters where the Constitution specifically provides for ordinary legislation.
A simple-majority law is not automatically a constitutional amendment under Article 368. Always check the specific constitutional provision.

Method 2 – Special Majority under Article 368

Most formal constitutional amendments require a special majority in each House of Parliament.

  • Majority of the total membership of that House; AND
  • Not less than two-thirds of the members present and voting.

Method 3 – Special Majority plus State Ratification

Amendments affecting specified federal provisions require:

  1. Special majority in each House of Parliament; and
  2. Ratification by the Legislatures of not less than one-half of the States.

These provisions protect the federal balance between the Union and the States.

3. Article 368 – Power of Parliament to Amend the Constitution

Article 368 is located in Part XX of the Constitution and deals with Parliament's constituent power and the procedure for constitutional amendments.

Article 368(1)

Parliament may, in exercise of its constituent power, amend any provision of the Constitution by way of addition, variation or repeal, in accordance with the procedure laid down in Article 368.

Article 368(2) – Amendment Bill Procedure

  1. A Constitutional Amendment Bill may be introduced in either House of Parliament.
  2. It can be introduced by a Minister or a private member.
  3. Prior permission of the President is not required for introduction.
  4. It must be passed separately in each House by the required special majority.
  5. If State ratification is required, the Bill must be ratified by at least half of the State Legislatures before it is presented to the President.
  6. After completion of the required procedure, the Bill is presented to the President.
  7. The President shall give assent to the Bill.

Meaning of Special Majority

Two conditions must be satisfied simultaneously in each House:

ConditionMeaning
Majority of total membershipMore than half of the total membership of the House.
Two-thirds of members present and votingAt least two-thirds of members who are present and actually vote.

Example: Suppose a House has a total membership of 500. More than 250 votes are needed under the total-membership condition. If 300 members are present and voting, at least 200 votes are needed under the two-thirds condition. Both conditions must be satisfied.

Important procedural rules

  • There is no provision for a joint sitting of Parliament to resolve a deadlock over a Constitutional Amendment Bill.
  • Both Houses must pass the Bill separately.
  • There is no provision for State Legislatures to initiate an Article 368 amendment Bill.
  • State Legislatures cannot amend the Bill; they can ratify or reject the proposal where ratification is required.
  • There is no constitutional deadline prescribed for State Legislatures to complete ratification.
  • After the Bill is passed in the required manner and presented, the President is constitutionally required to give assent.
Exam Point: Article 368 does not provide for a joint sitting. A disagreement between the two Houses cannot be resolved through a joint sitting under Article 108.

State Ratification – Key Rules

  • At least half of the States must ratify.
  • Ratification is by resolutions passed by the State Legislatures.
  • Where a State has a Legislative Council, ratification is by the State Legislature according to the constitutional scheme; the relevant resolution must be passed by the Legislative Assembly and, where applicable, the Legislative Council as prescribed for this purpose.
  • State ratification must be completed before the Bill is presented to the President for assent.
  • There is no requirement that every State must ratify.
Important: State ratification is not required for every constitutional amendment. It is required only when the amendment affects the specified federal provisions.

5. Evolution of Parliament's Amendment Power

The scope of Parliament's power to amend the Constitution has developed through important Supreme Court decisions and constitutional amendments.

1. Shankari Prasad v. Union of India (1951)

  • The Supreme Court upheld the First Constitutional Amendment.
  • It held that a constitutional amendment made under Article 368 was not a law within the meaning of Article 13 at that time.
  • Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights through the Article 368 procedure.

2. Sajjan Singh v. State of Rajasthan (1965)

  • The Supreme Court upheld Parliament's power to amend Fundamental Rights.
  • The judgment followed the broad approach adopted in Shankari Prasad.
  • Some judges expressed concerns about the possibility of damaging essential constitutional features.

3. Golaknath v. State of Punjab (1967)

  • The Supreme Court held that Parliament could not amend Fundamental Rights.
  • It treated a constitutional amendment as “law” for the purpose of Article 13.
  • The judgment introduced the doctrine of prospective overruling in this context.

4. 24th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1971

  • Inserted Article 13(4), stating that Article 13 does not apply to amendments made under Article 368.
  • Expressly stated Parliament's constituent power to amend the Constitution.
  • Changed the procedure relating to Presidential assent so that the President shall give assent to a duly passed amendment Bill.

5. Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

  • Decided by a 13-judge Bench of the Supreme Court.
  • By a narrow 7:6 majority, the Court held that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
  • However, Parliament cannot alter or destroy the Basic Structure of the Constitution.
  • This became known as the Basic Structure Doctrine.

6. 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976

  • Attempted to give Parliament's amending power an unlimited character.
  • Inserted clauses (4) and (5) into Article 368.
  • Attempted to exclude judicial review of constitutional amendments.

7. Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

  • The Supreme Court struck down the extension of unlimited amending power introduced by the 42nd Amendment.
  • It held that limited amending power is itself part of the Basic Structure.
  • It emphasised harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles.
  • Judicial review was recognised as an essential feature of the Constitution.

8. Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

  • The Supreme Court considered the relationship between the Ninth Schedule and the Basic Structure Doctrine.
  • It adopted 24 April 1973, the date of the Kesavananda Bharati judgment, as an important dividing line for Ninth Schedule insertions.
  • Post-Kesavananda insertions can be examined if they damage the Basic Structure.

9. I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

  • The Supreme Court held that laws inserted into the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 are subject to Basic Structure review.
  • Placing a law in the Ninth Schedule does not automatically immunise it from judicial scrutiny.
  • The Court examines whether the law damages or destroys the Basic Structure through its impact on constitutional rights and principles.

6. Basic Structure Doctrine

Meaning

The Basic Structure Doctrine means that Parliament can amend the Constitution under Article 368, but it cannot destroy or damage the essential features that form the identity of the Constitution.

తెలుగు: పార్లమెంట్ రాజ్యాంగంలోని అనేక అంశాలను సవరించగలదు. అయితే రాజ్యాంగం యొక్క ప్రాథమిక స్వరూపాన్ని నాశనం చేసే విధంగా సవరణ చేయలదు. దీనినే Basic Structure Doctrine అంటారు.

Is Basic Structure expressly listed in the Constitution?

No. The Constitution does not contain a single exhaustive list of Basic Structure features. The doctrine was developed by the Supreme Court through judicial interpretation, especially in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and later cases.

Why is the doctrine important?

  • Prevents constitutional authoritarianism.
  • Protects democratic institutions.
  • Preserves federal balance.
  • Protects judicial independence and judicial review.
  • Maintains continuity of constitutional identity while permitting change.

Kesavananda Bharati – Why is it important?

  • Largest Constitution Bench in Indian Supreme Court history: 13 judges.
  • Decision delivered on 24 April 1973.
  • Decision by 7:6 majority.
  • Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution.
  • Parliament cannot destroy or damage the Basic Structure.

Minerva Mills – Key Principle

Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is limited. A limited amending power is itself part of the Basic Structure. Judicial review cannot be removed in a way that destroys the constitutional scheme.

Basic Structure vs Ordinary Constitutional Provisions

  • Basic Structure refers to essential features that preserve constitutional identity.
  • Not every constitutional provision is automatically part of the Basic Structure.
  • Parliament can amend many constitutional provisions.
  • An amendment that damages or destroys the Basic Structure can be struck down by the Supreme Court.

10. Mains Answer Framework

Question: “Parliament's power to amend the Constitution is wide but not unlimited.” Discuss.

Introduction: Article 368 empowers Parliament to amend the Constitution through a special procedure. The amendment process combines flexibility and rigidity.

Scope of amending power:

  • Parliament can amend provisions of the Constitution, including Fundamental Rights.
  • Amendment Bills may be introduced in either House.
  • Most amendments require a special majority in both Houses.
  • Specified federal provisions require ratification by at least half of the States.

Constitutional limitations:

  • Kesavananda Bharati established the Basic Structure Doctrine.
  • Parliament cannot destroy the essential identity of the Constitution.
  • Minerva Mills reaffirmed limited amending power and judicial review.
  • I.R. Coelho confirmed that certain Ninth Schedule insertions remain subject to Basic Structure review.

Conclusion: Article 368 enables constitutional evolution, while the Basic Structure Doctrine protects constitutional identity. Together, they maintain a balance between democratic change and constitutional continuity.

Practice Mains Questions

  1. Explain the procedure for amendment of the Indian Constitution under Article 368.
  2. Discuss the three broad methods of constitutional amendment in India.
  3. Explain the Basic Structure Doctrine with reference to Kesavananda Bharati.
  4. Examine the significance of the 24th, 42nd and 44th Constitutional Amendments.
  5. Discuss the role of the Supreme Court in limiting Parliament's amending power.
  6. Why is State ratification required for certain constitutional amendments?
