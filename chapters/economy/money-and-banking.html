1. Meaning of Money – ద్రవ్యం అర్థం

Money is anything that is generally accepted as a medium of exchange for goods and services and for the settlement of debts.

తెలుగు: వస్తువులు, సేవల కొనుగోలు మరియు అప్పుల చెల్లింపుల కోసం సాధారణంగా అందరూ అంగీకరించే మారక సాధనాన్ని ద్రవ్యం అంటారు.

  • Money makes economic transactions easier.
  • It removes the difficulties of the barter system.
  • It provides a common unit for measuring prices.
  • It facilitates saving and future payments.
Important: Money is not limited to currency notes and coins. Bank deposits and certain other highly liquid assets are also included in broader measures of money.

Money developed over time as societies needed easier ways to exchange goods and services.

3. Barter System and Its Difficulties

The barter system is the direct exchange of one good or service for another without using money.

Problems of barter

  • Double coincidence of wants: Both parties must want what the other offers.
  • Lack of common measure: It is difficult to compare the values of different goods.
  • Difficulty in storing wealth: Some goods are perishable or difficult to store.
  • Indivisibility: Some goods cannot be divided into smaller units without losing value.
  • Deferred payments: Future obligations are difficult to express consistently.

తెలుగు: ఒక వ్యక్తి దగ్గర ఉన్న వస్తువు మరొకరికి కావాలి, అదే సమయంలో రెండో వ్యక్తి దగ్గర ఉన్న వస్తువు మొదటి వ్యక్తికి కావాలి. దీనినే Double Coincidence of Wants అంటారు.

4. Functions of Money – ద్రవ్యం విధులు

Primary Functions

  • Medium of Exchange: Money is used to buy and sell goods and services.
  • Measure of Value: Money provides a common unit to express prices.

Secondary Functions

  • Store of Value: Money can transfer purchasing power to the future, although inflation may reduce its real value.
  • Standard of Deferred Payments: Money allows future debts and payments to be expressed in common units.
  • Transfer of Value: Money makes it easier to transfer purchasing power between people and places.

తెలుగు: ద్రవ్యం మారక సాధనం, విలువ కొలమానం, విలువ నిల్వ సాధనం, భవిష్యత్ చెల్లింపుల ప్రమాణంగా పనిచేస్తుంది.

5. Types of Money – ద్రవ్యం రకాలు

  • Commodity Money: Money whose value is linked to the commodity from which it is made.
  • Fiat Money: Money that is accepted by law and monetary convention, rather than being redeemable for a fixed quantity of a commodity.
  • Legal Tender: Currency that must be accepted for payment of debts under applicable law, subject to legal conditions.
  • Credit Money: Money-like claims such as bank deposits that are used for payments.
  • Digital Money: Value represented and transferred electronically.
Indian currency notes are issued by the Reserve Bank of India, except the one-rupee note, which is issued by the Government of India. Coins are issued by the Government of India.

6. Money Supply – ద్రవ్య సరఫరా

Money supply refers to the stock of money available in an economy at a particular point in time. The Reserve Bank of India publishes monetary aggregates using defined components.

Important monetary aggregates in India

AggregateBroad description
M1Currency with the public + demand deposits with the banking system + other deposits with RBI
M2M1 + savings deposits with post office savings banks
M3M1 + time deposits with the banking system
M4M3 + total post office deposits, excluding National Savings Certificates
Exam Point: M1 is often called narrow money. M3 is commonly referred to as broad money in India.

These are conventional RBI monetary aggregates. The precise classification of deposits follows the RBI's published definitions.

7. Liquidity – ద్రవ్యత్వం

Liquidity refers to how easily an asset can be converted into money or used to make payments without significant loss of value.

  • Currency is highly liquid.
  • Demand deposits are highly liquid.
  • Time deposits are generally less liquid than demand deposits.
  • Property is usually less liquid because it may take time to sell.

తెలుగు: ఒక ఆస్తిని తక్కువ సమయంలో, తక్కువ నష్టంతో నగదుగా మార్చుకునే సామర్థ్యాన్ని ద్రవ్యత్వం అంటారు.

8. Central Bank – కేంద్ర బ్యాంకు

A central bank is the apex monetary authority of a country. It manages monetary policy, issues currency in accordance with law, supervises important aspects of the financial system and supports financial stability.

India's central bank is the Reserve Bank of India (RBI).

Reserve Bank of India – Important Facts

  • Established on 1 April 1935.
  • Established under the Reserve Bank of India Act, 1934.
  • Initially privately owned.
  • Nationalised in 1949.
  • Headquarters: Mumbai.
  • Acts as India's central bank and monetary authority.
Exam Point: RBI began operations on 1 April 1935 and was nationalised in 1949.

9. Main Functions of RBI – RBI ప్రధాన విధులు

  • Monetary Authority: Formulates and implements monetary policy to maintain price stability while keeping growth in mind.
  • Currency Issuer: Issues banknotes in India, except the one-rupee note.
  • Banker's Bank: Maintains accounts of banks and provides liquidity support under prescribed conditions.
  • Banker to Government: Performs banking functions for the Union and State Governments as agreed under law.
  • Regulator and Supervisor: Regulates and supervises banks and specified financial institutions.
  • Foreign Exchange Management: Manages foreign exchange reserves and administers relevant provisions of FEMA.
  • Payment System Oversight: Regulates and oversees payment and settlement systems under applicable law.
  • Lender of Last Resort: Can provide emergency liquidity support to eligible banks under specified conditions.

10. Commercial Banks – వాణిజ్య బ్యాంకులు

Commercial banks accept deposits from the public and provide loans and other financial services to individuals, businesses and institutions.

Main functions

  • Accepting deposits.
  • Providing loans and advances.
  • Facilitating payment and remittance services.
  • Providing cheque and collection services.
  • Offering digital banking and other financial services.

11. Credit Creation – రుణ సృష్టి

Commercial banks create deposit money when they provide loans. A loan is generally credited to a borrower's deposit account, increasing bank deposits in the banking system.

  • Banks retain a portion of deposits as reserves according to applicable requirements and their own liquidity needs.
  • The remaining funds may support lending.
  • Loans can generate additional deposits when spent and deposited into the banking system.
  • Actual credit creation is constrained by capital requirements, liquidity, risk, demand for loans and monetary regulation.

Simple Deposit Multiplier

In a simplified model:

Deposit Multiplier = 1 ÷ Required Reserve Ratio.

Example: If the required reserve ratio is 10%, the simple theoretical multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10. This is a simplified textbook model, not a guarantee that banks will create ten times the initial deposit in practice.

12. Monetary Policy – ద్రవ్య విధానం

Monetary policy refers to the actions taken by the central bank to influence interest rates, liquidity and credit conditions in order to achieve macroeconomic objectives.

Main objectives

  • Maintain price stability.
  • Support economic growth.
  • Manage liquidity in the banking system.
  • Support orderly credit conditions.
  • Contribute to financial stability.

In India, the RBI conducts monetary policy under the framework established by law.

13. Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

  • The MPC was established under the RBI Act framework through the 2016 amendment.
  • It determines the policy repo rate required to achieve the inflation target.
  • It has six members.
  • Three members are from the RBI, including the RBI Governor as Chairperson.
  • Three members are appointed by the Central Government.
  • The RBI Governor has a casting vote in case of a tie.
Exam Point: The inflation target is set by the Central Government in consultation with the RBI under the statutory framework. The MPC decides the policy rate needed to achieve it.

14. Repo Rate – రెపో రేటు

The repo rate is the policy rate at which the RBI provides short-term funds to eligible banks against eligible securities under the liquidity adjustment framework.

  • An increase in the repo rate generally makes borrowing from the RBI more expensive.
  • This can transmit into higher lending rates and reduce credit demand.
  • A reduction may make liquidity and credit conditions easier, depending on transmission.

తెలుగు: RBI బ్యాంకులకు అర్హమైన సెక్యూరిటీలకు బదులుగా స్వల్పకాలిక నిధులు అందించే విధానంలో ఉపయోగించే ముఖ్యమైన పాలసీ వడ్డీ రేటును Repo Rate అంటారు.

15. Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) and Reverse Repo

Standing Deposit Facility – SDF

SDF is a facility through which the RBI absorbs liquidity from banks without providing collateral. It is an important part of the liquidity management framework.

Reverse Repo Rate

Reverse repo is the rate associated with the RBI borrowing funds from banks against eligible collateral under the relevant framework.

In the current operating framework, SDF is the floor of the liquidity adjustment facility corridor, while the policy repo rate is the centre. The fixed-rate reverse repo remains an available instrument but is not the normal floor of the corridor.

16. Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) – నగదు నిల్వ నిష్పత్తి

CRR is the percentage of a bank's Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL) that it must maintain as a cash balance with the RBI.

  • CRR is maintained with the RBI.
  • It affects the amount of funds banks can use for lending.
  • An increase in CRR generally reduces lendable resources.
  • A reduction in CRR can increase liquidity available to banks.

తెలుగు: బ్యాంకులు తమ NDTLలో నిర్దిష్ట శాతాన్ని RBI వద్ద నగదు నిల్వగా ఉంచాలి. దానినే CRR అంటారు.

17. Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) – చట్టబద్ధ ద్రవ్య నిష్పత్తి

SLR is the percentage of a bank's NDTL that it must maintain in the form of specified liquid assets under the RBI's rules.

  • It is maintained by banks in prescribed forms such as cash, gold and eligible unencumbered securities.
  • It supports liquidity and financial stability.
  • An increase can reduce funds available for additional lending.

18. Bank Rate and Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)

Bank Rate

The Bank Rate is a rate at which the RBI is prepared to buy or rediscount eligible bills of exchange or other commercial papers under the statutory framework. It is also linked to certain penal rates.

Marginal Standing Facility

MSF allows eligible scheduled commercial banks to obtain overnight liquidity from the RBI against eligible securities, subject to prescribed conditions.

MSF is generally positioned above the policy repo rate in the liquidity adjustment facility corridor.

19. Quantitative and Qualitative Credit Controls

Quantitative tools

  • Repo rate and other policy rates.
  • CRR.
  • SLR.
  • Open Market Operations (OMO).
  • Liquidity adjustment operations.

Qualitative tools

  • Margin requirements.
  • Selective credit controls.
  • Moral suasion.
  • Directives and regulatory measures.

20. Open Market Operations (OMO)

Open Market Operations refer to the purchase and sale of government securities by the RBI in the open market to manage liquidity.

  • When RBI purchases government securities, it generally injects liquidity into the banking system.
  • When RBI sells government securities, it generally absorbs liquidity.
  • OMO is an important quantitative monetary policy instrument.

తెలుగు: RBI ప్రభుత్వ సెక్యూరిటీలను కొనుగోలు చేయడం లేదా విక్రయించడం ద్వారా బ్యాంకింగ్ వ్యవస్థలో ద్రవ్యత్వాన్ని నియంత్రిస్తుంది.

21. Inflation and Monetary Policy Connection

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. Monetary policy can influence demand and credit conditions to help manage inflation.

  • Higher policy rates can reduce borrowing and spending pressures over time.
  • Lower policy rates can support borrowing and investment when demand is weak.
  • Monetary policy works with time lags.
  • Supply-side inflation may not respond immediately to interest-rate changes.

22. Banking Structure in India

  • Public Sector Banks: Banks in which government ownership is controlling.
  • Private Sector Banks: Banks primarily owned by private shareholders.
  • Foreign Banks: Banks incorporated outside India but operating in India under regulatory permission.
  • Regional Rural Banks: Banks established to serve rural areas and priority groups.
  • Cooperative Banks: Member-based banking institutions operating under applicable regulatory frameworks.
  • Small Finance Banks: Banks focused on financial inclusion and providing basic banking services to underserved groups.
  • Payments Banks: Banks designed for small savings and payments/remittance services, subject to restrictions including limits on lending.

23. Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)

  • RRBs were established under the Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976.
  • The first RRBs began operations on 2 October 1975.
  • They aim to provide banking services in rural areas.
  • They support agriculture, small businesses and weaker sections.
  • They have a multi-agency ownership structure involving the Central Government, State Government and sponsor bank.

24. Financial Inclusion – ఆర్థిక సమ్మిళితత్వం

Financial inclusion means ensuring access to affordable and useful financial services for all sections of society, especially low-income and underserved groups.

  • Access to savings accounts.
  • Affordable credit.
  • Insurance and pension services.
  • Digital payment facilities.
  • Financial literacy and consumer protection.

Important initiatives

  • Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY): Promotes access to basic banking services.
  • Business Correspondents: Help banks provide services in remote and underserved locations.
  • Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT): Transfers eligible benefits directly to beneficiaries' bank accounts.
  • Priority Sector Lending: Encourages credit flow to specified sectors and weaker sections.

UPI

  • Developed by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI).
  • Enables instant bank-to-bank transfers.
  • Supports payments through mobile applications and other approved interfaces.
  • Can use a UPI ID, QR code or other supported payment details.

NEFT and RTGS

  • NEFT: Electronic funds transfer system operating on a 24x7 basis.
  • RTGS: Real-time gross settlement system, generally used for high-value transfers; the minimum amount is ₹2 lakh.

26. Priority Sector Lending (PSL)

Priority Sector Lending refers to the RBI's requirement for banks to provide a specified proportion of lending to identified priority sectors.

Important categories include:

  • Agriculture.
  • Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises (MSMEs).
  • Export credit under applicable rules.
  • Education.
  • Housing under specified conditions.
  • Social infrastructure.
  • Renewable energy.
  • Other eligible categories under RBI guidelines.

Objective: Ensure that important but underserved sectors receive adequate institutional credit.

27. Non-Performing Assets (NPA)

A Non-Performing Asset is a loan or advance that has stopped generating income for a bank as per the applicable prudential norms.

  • For most bank loans, an account is classified as NPA when interest or principal remains overdue for more than 90 days, subject to specific rules and exceptions.
  • NPAs affect bank profitability.
  • They reduce funds available for fresh lending.
  • They may weaken the financial health of banks.

Types of NPAs

  • Substandard Asset: An asset classified as NPA for a period up to the prescribed limit.
  • Doubtful Asset: An asset that has remained in the substandard category beyond the prescribed period.
  • Loss Asset: An asset identified as uncollectible or of little value, though it may not have been fully written off.

28. Banking Regulation and Deposit Protection

  • The Banking Regulation Act, 1949 provides an important legal framework for banking regulation in India.
  • The RBI regulates and supervises banks under applicable laws.
  • The Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC) provides deposit insurance under its statutory framework.
  • Eligible deposits are insured up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank, including principal and interest, subject to applicable rules and aggregation of accounts.
Exam Point: DICGC deposit insurance limit is ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank.

31. Mains Answer Framework

Question: Explain the role of the RBI in maintaining monetary and financial stability in India.

Introduction: The Reserve Bank of India is the country's central bank and plays a major role in monetary management and financial regulation.

Body:

  1. Monetary policy: Uses policy rates and liquidity tools to pursue price stability while keeping growth in mind.
  2. Banking regulation: Regulates and supervises banks and eligible financial institutions.
  3. Currency management: Issues and manages banknotes under the legal framework.
  4. Liquidity management: Uses CRR, OMO and liquidity facilities to manage banking-system liquidity.
  5. Financial stability: Monitors risks and strengthens confidence in the financial system.
  6. Payment systems: Oversees safe and efficient payment and settlement arrangements.

Conclusion: The RBI is essential for maintaining monetary stability, strengthening the banking system and supporting sustainable economic development.
