1. Meaning of National Income – జాతీయ ఆదాయం అర్థం

National income is the total income earned by the normal residents of a country from their productive economic activities during a specified period, usually one financial year.

తెలుగు: ఒక దేశానికి చెందిన సాధారణ నివాసితులు ఒక నిర్దిష్ట కాలంలో ఉత్పత్తి కార్యకలాపాల ద్వారా సంపాదించిన ఆదాయాల మొత్తాన్ని జాతీయ ఆదాయం అంటారు.

  • It measures the income generated through economic activity.
  • It helps assess the size and performance of an economy.
  • It is used to study economic growth and changes in living standards.
  • It supports government planning and policy decisions.
Important: National income is a flow concept measured over a period of time, not a stock measured at a single point in time.

2. National Income and Related Concepts

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) – స్థూల దేశీయోత్పత్తి

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country during a given period.

తెలుగు: ఒక దేశ భౌగోళిక ఆర్థిక పరిధిలో ఒక నిర్దిష్ట కాలంలో ఉత్పత్తి చేసిన తుది వస్తువులు, సేవల మార్కెట్ విలువ GDP.

  • It is based on domestic territory.
  • It includes production by domestic and foreign-owned production units located within the country.
  • It counts final goods and services to avoid double counting.

Gross National Product (GNP) – స్థూల జాతీయోత్పత్తి

GNP measures the value of final output attributable to the normal residents of a country, including net factor income from abroad.

Formula: GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA).

Net Domestic Product (NDP)

NDP is GDP after deducting depreciation or consumption of fixed capital.

Formula: NDP = GDP − Depreciation.

Net National Product (NNP)

NNP is GNP after deducting depreciation.

Formula: NNP = GNP − Depreciation.

3. Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)

NFIA is the difference between factor income received by residents from abroad and factor income paid to non-residents.

Formula: NFIA = Factor income received from abroad − Factor income paid abroad.

  • If receipts from abroad are greater than payments abroad, NFIA is positive.
  • If payments abroad are greater than receipts, NFIA is negative.
  • It connects domestic production measures with national income measures.
Example: If residents receive ₹100 crore in factor income from abroad and pay ₹70 crore to foreign residents, NFIA = ₹30 crore.

4. Depreciation – తరుగుదల

Depreciation is the reduction in the value of fixed assets due to wear and tear, ageing and obsolescence during production.

తెలుగు: యంత్రాలు, భవనాలు వంటి స్థిర మూలధన వస్తువులు వాడకం, కాలం గడవడం వల్ల కోల్పోయే విలువను తరుగుదల అంటారు.

  • It is also called consumption of fixed capital in national accounts.
  • Gross measures include depreciation.
  • Net measures deduct depreciation.
Remember: Gross − Depreciation = Net.

5. Market Price and Factor Cost

Market price is the price paid by the buyer. Factor cost represents the income received by factors of production for their contribution to production.

  • Indirect taxes generally increase the market price relative to factor cost.
  • Subsidies reduce the market price relative to factor cost.

Formula: Factor Cost = Market Price − Indirect Taxes + Subsidies.

Formula: Market Price = Factor Cost + Indirect Taxes − Subsidies.

Modern national accounts commonly emphasise GDP at market prices and Gross Value Added (GVA) at basic prices. Older exam materials may use GDP at factor cost and NNP at factor cost as the traditional national-income measure. Understand the accounting framework and the terminology used in the question.

6. National Income – Traditional Definition

In traditional Indian economics textbooks, National Income is often described as NNP at factor cost.

Traditional formula: National Income = NNP at Factor Cost.

This traditional expression is useful for competitive examinations. In modern national accounting, published aggregates and valuation conventions have evolved, so always identify whether a question refers to the traditional or current framework.

7. Three Methods of Measuring National Income

A. Production Method / Value Added Method

This method calculates the value added by all producing units during a given period.

Value Added = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption.

  • Output produced by agriculture, industry and services is considered.
  • Intermediate goods are deducted to prevent double counting.
  • Value added across production units is aggregated.

తెలుగు: ఉత్పత్తి విలువ నుంచి మధ్యంతర వినియోగాన్ని తీసివేస్తే Value Added వస్తుంది.

B. Income Method

This method adds factor incomes generated through production.

  • Compensation of employees (wages and salaries).
  • Rent and other property income, where applicable.
  • Interest and other factor income, as defined in the accounts.
  • Profits and mixed income of self-employed persons.

తెలుగు: ఉత్పత్తి ప్రక్రియలో శ్రమ, భూమి, మూలధనం, వ్యవస్థాపకత ద్వారా వచ్చిన ఆదాయాలను కలిపి లెక్కిస్తారు.

C. Expenditure Method

This method measures final expenditure on goods and services produced in the economy.

GDP = C + I + G + (X − M)

SymbolMeaning
CPrivate final consumption expenditure
IInvestment / Gross capital formation
GGovernment final consumption expenditure
XExports
MImports

Imports are subtracted because they may be included in consumption, investment or government spending but were not produced domestically.

8. Double Counting – ద్వంద్వ లెక్కింపు

Double counting occurs when the value of the same product is counted more than once at different stages of production.

Example: Wheat is sold to a flour mill, flour is sold to a bakery, and bread is sold to consumers. Adding the full value of wheat, flour and bread would count some value repeatedly.

  • Use value added at each production stage.
  • Alternatively, count only final goods and services.
  • Intermediate consumption must not be added separately to final output.
Exam Point: Value Added Method helps avoid double counting.

9. Nominal GDP and Real GDP

Nominal GDP – ప్రస్తుత ధరల GDP

Nominal GDP measures output using the prices prevailing in the same period.

Real GDP – స్థిర ధరల GDP

Real GDP measures output using prices of a chosen base year, helping separate changes in output from changes in prices.

Nominal GDPReal GDP
Current pricesConstant/base-year prices
Affected by output and price changesDesigned to measure volume/output changes
May rise due to inflationAdjusts for price-level changes
If nominal GDP increases, it does not automatically mean real production has increased. Prices may also have risen.

10. GDP Deflator – GDP డిఫ్లేటర్

The GDP deflator is a price index that measures the price level of all domestically produced final goods and services included in GDP.

Formula:

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100.

It reflects price changes in the composition of domestic output and is broader in coverage than a consumer price index.

Example: Nominal GDP = ₹600 lakh crore; Real GDP = ₹500 lakh crore. GDP Deflator = (600 ÷ 500) × 100 = 120.

11. Per Capita Income – తలసరి ఆదాయం

Per capita income is an average measure of income per person.

Formula: Per Capita Income = National Income ÷ Population.

  • It helps compare average income across countries or regions.
  • It does not show how income is distributed among people.
  • High per capita income does not automatically mean low poverty or equality.

12. Personal Income and Disposable Income

Personal Income

Personal income is the income actually received by individuals and households from different sources before personal direct taxes.

Disposable Personal Income

Disposable personal income is the income available to households after personal direct taxes.

Formula: Disposable Personal Income = Personal Income − Personal Direct Taxes.

It can be used for consumption and saving.

13. GDP, Welfare and Limitations

GDP is an important indicator of economic activity, but it is not a complete measure of people's welfare.

Limitations

  • It does not directly measure income inequality.
  • Unpaid household work may not be included in conventional GDP.
  • Environmental damage may not be fully reflected.
  • It does not directly measure health, education or quality of life.
  • Informal and non-market activities may be difficult to measure.
  • GDP per capita is an average and can hide unequal distribution.
  • It does not measure leisure or all aspects of human well-being.
GDP measures production, not every dimension of development. Human Development Index (HDI) includes health, education and income dimensions.

14. National Income Accounting in India

  • The National Statistical Office (NSO), under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), is responsible for compiling and publishing national accounts estimates.
  • National accounts provide estimates of GDP, GVA, consumption, capital formation and related aggregates.
  • Estimates are revised as more complete data becomes available.
  • GDP estimates are released as advance, provisional and revised estimates according to the official statistical calendar and methodology.
  • Base years are periodically updated to reflect changes in the structure of the economy and data availability.
Exam Point: NSO is the key official agency for India's national accounts statistics. It functions under MoSPI.

15. GDP and GVA – Important Difference

Gross Value Added (GVA) measures the value generated by producers after deducting intermediate consumption from output.

GVA = Value of Output − Intermediate Consumption.

At the aggregate economy level, GDP at market prices can be derived from GVA at basic prices by adding product taxes and subtracting product subsidies.

GDP at Market Prices = GVA at Basic Prices + Product Taxes − Product Subsidies.

GVA helps analyse sector-wise contribution to economic activity, while GDP provides an aggregate measure of final domestic production.

18. Mains Answer Framework

Question: Explain the importance and limitations of GDP as an indicator of economic development.

Introduction: Gross Domestic Product measures the market value of final goods and services produced within a country's domestic territory during a specified period.

Importance:

  1. Measures the size and performance of the economy.
  2. Helps assess economic growth over time through real GDP.
  3. Supports policy formulation and economic planning.
  4. Enables sectoral and international comparisons, subject to limitations.
  5. Helps analyse consumption, investment and production trends.

Limitations:

  1. Does not show income distribution.
  2. Does not fully capture unpaid household work.
  3. Does not directly measure environmental sustainability.
  4. Does not fully represent health, education and quality of life.
  5. May not capture all informal economic activity accurately.

Conclusion: GDP is an essential measure of economic activity, but it should be used alongside indicators of human development, inequality and environmental sustainability.
